Steering Wheel Shaking at Speed? What Causes It and What to Check

Edwin·Master Automotive Technician·November 10, 2026·Edwin Garage, Ang Mo Kio

A steering wheel that shakes at higher speeds can be caused by wheel imbalance, tyre damage, a bent rim or worn steering and suspension parts. Symptoms alone cannot establish the cause or whether it is safe to drive, even when the shake is light. Reduce speed smoothly without abrupt steering or braking and stop somewhere safe if the vibration is new, worsening or unexplained. A sudden or severe shake, impact, tyre bulge, pressure loss, loose steering, strong pulling, wheel noise or reduced braking means do not continue: arrange recovery.

When should you stop driving straight away?

Slow down, pull over somewhere safe and switch on your hazard lights if you notice the shake together with any of these:

  • A sudden, violent shake that started right after hitting a kerb, pothole or road debris.
  • A bulge or cut on the side of a tyre. Our tyre sidewall bulge guide explains why this is not safe to drive on.
  • The car pulling hard to one side, or the steering feeling loose or vague.
  • A rumbling, grinding or knocking noise from one wheel. See wheel bearing noise.
  • A red warning light, or a burning smell from the wheels after driving.

If the car is not safe to drive, arrange recovery instead of driving it to the workshop. The breakdown and towing guide explains what to do next.

Wheel balance: the most common cause

A tyre and wheel never weigh exactly the same all the way around. Small weights are fixed to the rim so the wheel spins evenly. If a weight falls off, or a tyre is fitted without being balanced properly, the wheel wobbles slightly as it spins. At low speed you will not feel it, but at higher speeds the wobble becomes a steady shake in the steering wheel. A workshop corrects this by spinning the wheel on a balancing machine and refitting the weights.

Tyre and rim damage

A tyre with an internal fault, a flat spot, uneven wear or a bulge can make the car shake. So can a rim bent by an impact. Only inspect externally when safely parked away from traffic; do not crawl under the car or touch hot components. Check cold pressures against the vehicle placard when safe, but a normal pressure reading and no visible damage do not exclude an internal tyre fault. Do not drive on a bulging tyre or restart a journey simply to test whether the shake remains.

Alignment and worn suspension parts

Wheel alignment affects how evenly the tyres wear and how the car tracks on the road. Worn suspension and steering parts let the wheel move when it should be held firmly, and you may feel this as a shake or a loose steering feel. Our guide to suspension noises lists the usual signs. These parts affect vehicle control. Loose steering, strong pulling or a new shake after an impact requires safe stopping and recovery; do not drive on to test it.

What to do in the first few minutes

  1. Slow down and drive smoothly. Avoid hard braking and sharp turns.
  2. If any stop sign above applies, pull over safely, switch on your hazard lights and arrange recovery.
  3. Describe only what you have already noticed: the approximate speed range, where the vibration was felt and whether it changed during normal braking. Do not reproduce it at speed, deliberately accelerate or use a phone while driving.
  4. Only when safely parked away from traffic, look externally for tyre damage. Do not step into live traffic, crawl underneath or assume a normal-looking tyre is safe. Leave roadside checks to recovery personnel when the location is unsafe.
  5. Tell the workshop if it began after a new tyre, a kerb hit or a pothole.

What a workshop checks

To find the cause, a mechanic will usually:

  • Carry out static tyre, wheel-security and steering/suspension safety checks first. A controlled road test is considered only if appropriate after those checks, not by deliberately driving an unsafe vehicle at the problem speed.
  • Inspect the tyres and rims for bulges, damage and uneven wear.
  • Check wheel balance on a balancing machine and look for missing weights.
  • Check wheel alignment.
  • Inspect ball joints, tie rod ends, bushes and wheel bearings for play.
  • Check the brake discs and pads if the shake happens when braking.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my steering wheel shake only at higher speeds?

Wheel imbalance is a common possibility, but tyre damage, rim runout, mounting problems and worn steering or suspension parts can feel similar. Some faults become more noticeable within a speed range. Symptoms alone cannot identify the cause or establish safety, so inspection is needed before further high-speed driving.

Is it safe to drive with a shaking steering wheel?

A light or gradual shake is not proof that driving is safe. Reduce speed smoothly and stop somewhere safe if vibration is new, worsening or unexplained; seek advice before continuing. A sudden or severe shake, an impact, tyre bulge or pressure loss, loose steering, strong pulling, wheel noise or reduced braking means do not continue: arrange recovery. Do not deliberately reproduce the vibration at speed.

Is it wheel balancing or wheel alignment?

They fix different problems. Wheel balancing corrects uneven weight around the wheel and tyre, which usually shows up as a shake at certain speeds. Wheel alignment corrects the angles of the wheels, which usually shows up as the car pulling to one side or uneven tyre wear. A workshop can check both.

Why does the steering wheel shake only when I brake?

Brake-disc thickness variation or runout, hub condition, caliper faults and worn suspension can contribute. Braking-related vibration does not identify one failed part or completely rule out wheel and tyre faults. Arrange prompt assessment; reduced braking, strong pulling, severe vibration, smoke or burning smell means stop safely and arrange recovery.

Can worn suspension parts make the steering shake?

Yes. Worn ball joints, tie rod ends, bushes or wheel bearings can let the wheel move more than it should, which you may feel as a shake, a loose steering feel or a knocking sound. These parts are safety-related, so have them inspected.

What should I tell the workshop about the steering shake?

Tell them the speed range where it happens, whether it is in the steering wheel or the seat, whether it changes when you brake or accelerate, and whether it began after a new tyre, a kerb hit or a pothole. A short note on the date it started helps.

Key Takeaways

  • Wheel imbalance is a common possibility, but symptoms alone cannot establish cause or safety.
  • Tyre damage, a bent rim, alignment or worn suspension parts can feel the same.
  • Braking-related shake needs brake, hub and suspension assessment; timing alone is not a diagnosis.
  • A sudden or severe shake, impact, tyre damage, loose steering or reduced braking means stop safely and arrange recovery.
  • Tell the workshop the speed range and whether it began after a kerb hit, pothole or new tyre.
