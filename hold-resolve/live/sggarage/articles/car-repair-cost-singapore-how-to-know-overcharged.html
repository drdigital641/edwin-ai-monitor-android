Why Car Repair Overcharging Happens in Singapore

Singapore has hundreds of workshops, from authorised dealers to neighbourhood indies. The quality of honesty varies widely. Overcharging typically happens in three ways:

  • Parts upselling: Recommending premium parts when standard OEM would suffice — or recommending replacement of parts that are still serviceable
  • Labour padding: Quoting excessive hours for jobs that experienced mechanics complete in half the time
  • Ghost work: Charging for services on the invoice that were never actually performed
  • Bundling: Insisting that unrelated items must be replaced together when only one item needs attention
  • Diagnostic fishing: Running extensive (and expensive) scans before giving you any information, then using the "findings" to justify a large repair bill

None of these are exclusive to any one type of workshop. We have seen inflated quotes from high-end dealers and from small workshops alike. The common thread is almost always the same — the customer did not have enough information to push back.

Warning Signs Your Quote May Be Inflated

Here is what to watch for when you receive a repair estimate:

  • No itemised breakdown: A good quote shows parts cost and labour cost separately. A lump-sum figure with no detail is a red flag.
  • Pressure to decide immediately: Honest workshops explain what is urgent vs. what can wait. High-pressure tactics — "we need to order the part today" — should raise your antennae.
  • Replacing instead of repairing: Some worn parts can be serviced or rebuilt rather than replaced outright. If a workshop jumps straight to full replacement without discussing repair options, ask why.
  • Charges for multiple diagnosis sessions: You should only pay for diagnosis once. If a workshop wants to charge you again because the "first scan didn't find it", that is not your problem.
  • Vague warranty terms: A reputable workshop backs its work with a clear warranty on both parts and labour. If they cannot tell you what the warranty covers, walk away.

What a Fair Car Repair Quote Looks Like

A transparent quote from an honest workshop should include:

  1. Fault description: What exactly is wrong, explained in plain language
  2. Parts list: Name and quantity of each part, with a choice of OEM, aftermarket, or remanufactured where applicable
  3. Labour: What work the job involves and whether labour is included in the price (at Edwin Garage, every quoted price includes parts, labour and GST)
  4. What is urgent vs. advisory: Items that must be fixed now versus items to monitor at the next service
  5. Warranty statement: Minimum 3 months on parts and labour is reasonable; many reputable workshops offer 6–12 months

How to Protect Yourself: Practical Steps

Here is what we recommend to every driver before they hand over their keys:

  • Request a written quote before authorising work. Never give verbal approval for repairs you have not seen itemised in writing.
  • Ask to see the worn parts. A good workshop will show you the old component when they replace it. If they cannot, ask why.
  • Do not authorise the full job sight-unseen. Approve diagnosis first, then review the quote, then approve the repair. These are three separate steps.
  • Check if it is urgent or advisory. Some items are safety-critical (brakes, tyres, steering); others can wait a few thousand km. Know which is which.
  • Get a second opinion for big jobs. Anything above $500 is worth a 15-minute check at another workshop before committing.

Is it normal to pay a diagnosis fee in Singapore?

Yes — a diagnosis fee is normal, particularly for electrical or intermittent faults. Diagnosis at Edwin Garage is $30 if you don't go ahead with the repair. The issue arises when workshops charge multiple diagnosis fees, or when the fee is disproportionately high relative to the repair cost.

Should I always go with the cheapest quote?

Not necessarily. The cheapest quote may use inferior parts, skip proper diagnosis, or cut corners on labour. Focus on value: a clear itemised breakdown, genuine OEM or quality aftermarket parts, and a solid warranty on both parts and labour. The cheapest quote often costs more in the long run when the job needs redoing.

How do I know if a part recommendation is genuine?

Ask to see the old part when it is removed. Ask the mechanic to explain what failed and show you the wear or damage. A trustworthy workshop will walk you through this without hesitation. If they cannot — or will not — show you the worn component, that is a serious concern.

What should I do if I suspect I have already been overcharged?

First, request an itemised invoice if you do not already have one. If the workshop refuses, that itself tells you something. You can also contact CASE (Consumers Association of Singapore) for mediation. Going forward, build a relationship with a workshop that gives you transparent quotes before every job — not after.

Does car make or age affect how much workshops charge?

Yes. European cars (Mercedes, BMW, Audi) generally cost more to service because parts are pricier and some procedures require specialist tools. Older cars may have higher labour costs if components are corroded or harder to access. That said, you should still receive an itemised quote — the parts and labour explanation remains the same regardless of make or age. Common Misconceptions About Car Repair Pricing

Myth: Authorised dealers always have better quality. Authorised dealers use manufacturer-certified parts and procedures, which is important when your car is under warranty. But for cars out of warranty, independent workshops using OEM-equivalent parts deliver identical quality at 30–60% lower cost. The key is finding an independent workshop that is genuinely skilled — not cutting corners.

Myth: If it's expensive, the parts must be better. High markup on labour and parts does not equal higher quality. Some workshops mark up generic parts at premium prices. Always ask what brand of parts is being used and whether OEM alternatives are available.

Myth: You have to fix everything the workshop recommends. Recommendations are exactly that — recommendations. Safety-critical items (failed brakes, severely worn tyres, steering faults) should not be deferred. Everything else can be discussed, prioritised, and scheduled on your terms.
