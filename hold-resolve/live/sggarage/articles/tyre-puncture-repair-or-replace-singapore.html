Tyre Puncture Repair or Replacement in Singapore?

Edwin · Master Automotive Technician · November 1, 2026 · Edwin Garage, Ang Mo Kio

A tyre puncture may be repairable if the damage is confined to an approved tread repair area and the tyre has no disqualifying damage. A sidewall puncture, bulge or damage from driving underinflated usually means replacement. The final decision requires the tyre to be removed and inspected internally—not just a photograph of the screw.

At Edwin Garage, we want drivers to understand the decision before approving work. Our car servicing and repair workshop in Ang Mo Kio can help assess tyre-related concerns and the appropriate next step. This guide focuses on puncture repair versus replacement, rather than general tyre lifespan. A small visible hole does not necessarily mean a small repair.

What counts as a tyre puncture?

A puncture is a penetration through the tyre that can let pressurised air escape. A nail or screw may remain embedded and partly restrict the leak, so the tyre can look normal initially. Air loss can also come from the valve, wheel rim, bead seal or a damaged pressure sensor assembly. Finding a screw does not prove it is the only problem.

A proper repair must address the injury through the rubber and restore the inner air seal, using a method approved for that tyre and repair system. An external plug alone does not provide the internal inspection needed to rule out hidden damage. The tyre maker's restrictions matter, including those for run-flat, acoustic-foam or self-sealing designs.

Warning signs: slow leak or stop-driving problem?

  • Repeated pressure loss: one tyre needs topping up more often than the others, even without a visible object.
  • Pressure warning: a dashboard alert needs checking against actual pressures and the vehicle handbook, not simply resetting.
  • Visible flattening, thumping or steering change: slow smoothly and stop somewhere safe. Avoid sharp braking or steering where possible.
  • Sidewall bulge, split or exposed cords: do not treat this as an ordinary patchable hole.

If the tyre is flat, rapidly losing pressure or visibly damaged, do not drive to our workshop just because Ang Mo Kio is nearby. Arrange roadside assistance or recovery. On an expressway, prioritise reaching a safe stopping position and keeping people away from traffic; do not attempt a roadside tyre change in an exposed lane. Follow the road operator's emergency instructions.

For the separate structural-damage problem, see our guide to a tyre sidewall bulge and safe driving decisions. Inflating a bulging tyre does not repair its structure.

Why pressure drops after a nail or screw enters

Road debris can penetrate the tread while the rubber flexes around it. The leak rate depends on the injury, object angle and movement—not simply the screw head's size. Debris around construction access points and carparks can be a risk, but the route you drove cannot establish the cause by itself.

Other possibilities include a leaking valve core, corroded or damaged wheel sealing surface, an old repair that leaks, or impact damage after a pothole or kerb strike. Temperature changes affect pressure readings too. Compare cold readings using a reliable gauge and the vehicle's specified pressures, rather than assuming every change is a puncture.

Singapore's hot afternoons and heavy rain do not make a damaged tyre self-seal safely. Avoid repeated top-ups as a substitute for finding a persistent leak. If you notice a noise rather than pressure loss, our wheel bearing versus tyre noise guide explains why similar sounds need different checks.

When repair may be reasonable—and when it is not

A repair candidate generally has a limited injury within the tyre manufacturer's permitted tread repair area, usable tread and no structural or internal damage. Injury size, angle, previous repairs and spacing all matter. We would not approve repair solely because the screw appears to sit in the middle of the tread. The actual channel can travel at an angle.

Shoulder and sidewall injuries, exposed cords, bulges, serious cuts, bead damage or evidence of running flat can rule out repair. A tyre that is excessively worn or deteriorated may also be unsuitable. A generous-looking tread depth does not override damaged internal cords or an unserviceable inner liner.

Run-flat tyres require particular care. Whether repair is permitted depends on the tyre maker and the circumstances of operation after pressure loss. The driver's estimated distance is useful history, not proof of internal condition. Do not apply a universal repair rule or a generic driving-distance allowance to every run-flat tyre.

What the workshop needs to inspect

  1. Take the history. Explain when the warning appeared, any top-ups, sealant use and whether the car was driven while flat or very soft.
  2. Locate the leak. Check the tyre, valve and wheel sealing areas using an appropriate leak test. Do not remove an embedded screw at home to find out what happens.
  3. Inspect inside. With approval, remove the tyre from the wheel to examine the injury channel, inner liner, sidewalls and bead for hidden damage.
  4. Explain the decision. Identify why repair is acceptable under the relevant guidance, or show the damage that makes replacement necessary.
  5. Finish and verify. After approved work, check sealing, correct pressure and wheel balance as applicable. Handle any pressure-monitoring reset or relearn according to the vehicle procedure.

We prefer an explanation that separates observation from recommendation: “the inner sidewall shows damage” is more useful than “all punctures need a new tyre”. Equally, repair should not be promised before inspection. Ask for the finding and any limitations to be recorded on the job documentation.

If replacement is needed, must you buy two tyres?

Not automatically. The decision depends on tyre specification, remaining tread and condition across the axle, vehicle requirements and drivetrain tolerances. Some all-wheel-drive vehicles have strict requirements on differences in rolling circumference. Matching only the printed tyre size may not be sufficient.

Ask which replacement meets the required size, load rating, speed rating and construction, and whether a single replacement is suitable for your vehicle. Seek a clear reason for a pair or set rather than accepting either “always one” or “always four”. If there was an impact or uneven wear, wheel alignment checks may be relevant. A puncture alone does not automatically require alignment.

Cost factors: compare the complete SGD scope

Request an itemised estimate in SGD. For repair, clarify whether removal, internal inspection, the approved repair, refitting, balancing, valve work and leak verification are included. Ask whether an inspection charge remains payable if the tyre cannot be repaired. For replacement, compare the actual tyre specification, fitting, balancing, disposal and any pressure-sensor work.

There is no defensible single price for every puncture. A valve leak and a damaged run-flat tyre are different jobs. Agree the assessment scope before work begins; our guide to diagnostic fees and approval boundaries explains how to avoid confusing an initial check with unlimited investigation. Do not choose a structurally unsuitable repair simply because it costs less today.

Prevention and common misconceptions

Check cold pressures at the interval in your handbook and before longer trips, using the vehicle placard rather than the maximum pressure on the tyre sidewall. Inspect for cuts, bulges and unusual wear without crawling under an unsupported vehicle. Avoid mounting kerbs and driving over visible debris where it is safe to do so.

“It holds air, so it is safe” is not a reliable test. A tyre may retain pressure after structural damage. “Sealant fixes every puncture” is also wrong: a mobility kit is a temporary measure only where the vehicle's instructions allow it. Tell the workshop if sealant was used. Never exceed the kit's stated restrictions, and do not use it to justify driving on sidewall damage.

Can a screw in the tyre always be repaired?

No. Repairability depends on the injury location, size and angle, tyre condition, previous repairs and manufacturer restrictions. Removal and internal inspection are needed before confirming whether an approved repair is appropriate.

Can I drive with a slow puncture?

A slow leak is not permission to keep driving. If the tyre cannot maintain the specified pressure, is visibly damaged or changes the car's handling, stop safely and arrange assistance. A photograph or pressure top-up cannot confirm internal safety.

Is an external tyre plug a permanent repair?

An external plug alone does not provide the internal inspection or complete repair assessment required. Follow the tyre manufacturer's guidance and an approved repair system; do not assume a roadside plug makes every tyre suitable for continued use.

Can run-flat tyres be repaired after a puncture?

Sometimes a manufacturer may permit repair under specific conditions, while other tyres or circumstances rule it out. The tyre's instructions, pressure-loss history and internal condition must be checked. There is no universal yes-or-no rule for all run-flat tyres.

Does a puncture mean I need wheel alignment?

Not by itself. Alignment may be appropriate after an impact, with uneven wear or when steering symptoms suggest a geometry problem. Puncture repair and wheel balancing address different issues from wheel alignment.
