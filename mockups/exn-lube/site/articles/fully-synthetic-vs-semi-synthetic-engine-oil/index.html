Fully Synthetic vs Semi-Synthetic Engine Oil

By the EXN Lube technical team · 8 November 2026

Mineral oil is refined from crude oil. Semi-synthetic oil blends mineral and synthetic base oils. Fully synthetic oil uses engineered base stocks such as PAO and esters. Fully synthetic oil usually resists heat and ageing better, which helps in hot climates and turbo engines, but it costs more. Always match the grade your manual lists.

What is mineral engine oil?

Mineral oil is a base oil refined directly from crude oil. It lubricates well and has long been used in simple, older engines. Its molecules vary in size, so it can thin out and oxidise faster under high heat than a fully synthetic base. It is usually the lowest-cost option.

What is semi-synthetic engine oil?

Semi-synthetic oil is a blend of mineral and synthetic base oils plus additives. It aims to give better heat resistance and cleanliness than mineral oil, at a cost between mineral and fully synthetic. The exact share of each base oil differs by brand and product.

What is fully synthetic engine oil?

Fully synthetic oil is made from engineered base stocks with a more uniform molecular structure. This gives it steadier flow across a wide temperature range and better resistance to oxidation. Two common base stocks are PAO and esters.

What are PAO and ester base oils?

PAO and esters are two kinds of synthetic base stock used in high-performance engine oils.

  • PAO (polyalphaolefin) is built from small, uniform molecules. It stays fluid in the cold and holds up well at high temperature.
  • Esters are polar molecules that cling to metal surfaces. They add stability under heat and can help keep deposits down. They are often combined with PAO.

Which oil suits a hot, tropical climate?

Fully synthetic oil is often a good fit for hot, tropical conditions. Malaysian traffic means long idling, stop-go driving and high under-bonnet temperatures. Heat speeds up oil ageing, and a fully synthetic base resists that better than mineral oil. Check that the viscosity grade still matches your manual.

Do turbo engines need fully synthetic oil?

Many turbo engines call for fully synthetic oil, because a turbocharger runs very hot and puts extra stress on the oil. Makers often write the requirement in the manual. If yours does, follow it. If the manual allows more than one type, fully synthetic is the safer match for hard use.

What about older engines?

An older engine can usually run the oil type its maker listed at the time, and a modern synthetic of the right grade is often suitable too. Worn engines with leaks may behave differently with a new oil type, so talk to your workshop before switching. Do not change grade just because the oil is synthetic.

Is fully synthetic oil worth the extra cost?

It depends on your engine and how you drive. Fully synthetic oil costs more per litre, but it can resist ageing better. It may suit you if you have a modern or turbo engine, drive in heavy traffic or want longer protection between changes, provided your maker's interval is followed. If your manual allows semi-synthetic and your use is light, that can also be a sound choice.

How do I choose an engine oil type?

  1. Read your owner's manual for the viscosity grade and specification.
  2. Check whether the maker requires fully synthetic oil.
  3. Think about your driving: short trips, heavy traffic, towing or long highway runs.
  4. Match the label to the manual, including API and ACEA ratings.
  5. Follow the change interval, and shorten it for severe use if your workshop advises.

Questions and answers

Is fully synthetic oil better than semi-synthetic?

Fully synthetic oil generally resists heat and ageing better, but it costs more. Semi-synthetic can be fine if your manual allows it and your driving is light. The right choice is the one that meets your manual's grade and specification.

What is PAO in engine oil?

PAO stands for polyalphaolefin, a synthetic base oil made from small, uniform molecules. It flows well in the cold and stays stable at high temperature. It is often used in fully synthetic engine oils, sometimes with esters.

Can I switch from semi-synthetic to fully synthetic oil?

In most cases yes, as long as the viscosity grade and specification match your manual. For older or leaking engines, ask your workshop first, since a different oil type may change how seals and deposits behave.
