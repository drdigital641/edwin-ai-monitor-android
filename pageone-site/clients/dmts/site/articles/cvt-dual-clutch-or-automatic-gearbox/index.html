CVT, Dual-Clutch or Automatic: Which Gearbox Does Your Car Have?

CVT, dual-clutch (DSG) or conventional automatic? How to tell which gearbox your car has, how each works, its typical problems, and why it matters for repair.

By Jacky Gearbox · 15 October 2026

Most automatic cars in Singapore use one of three gearbox types: a conventional automatic with a torque converter, a CVT (continuously variable transmission) or a dual-clutch gearbox (Volkswagen group cars call theirs DSG). The quickest way to know yours is the owner's manual or the car's specification sheet. The type matters because each one fails differently and needs its own fluid.

What is a conventional automatic gearbox?

A conventional automatic connects to the engine through a torque converter, a fluid coupling that lets the car stop in gear without stalling. Inside are sets of gears, clutch packs and a valve body with solenoids that choose the gear. It is the oldest of the three designs and is used in many cars, from small family cars to large saloons.

What is a CVT?

A CVT has no fixed gears. It uses a steel belt or chain running between two pulleys that change size, so the ratio changes smoothly. When you accelerate, the engine speed often stays steady while the car speeds up, instead of rising and dropping with each gear change. Many Japanese cars use a CVT. Some hybrids use a different design that is also called an e-CVT, so check what your car has.

What is a dual-clutch (DSG) gearbox?

A dual-clutch gearbox is like two manual gearboxes in one, with one clutch for the odd gears and one for the even gears. A control unit, often called the mechatronic unit, changes gear by switching between the two clutches, so gear changes are very quick. Some dual-clutch gearboxes run their clutches in oil (wet), others run them dry.

How can I tell which gearbox my car has?

  • Check the owner's manual or the car's specification sheet for the transmission type
  • Search the make, model and year together with the word "transmission"
  • Notice how it drives: a CVT has no gear steps, a dual-clutch shifts very quickly, a conventional automatic has smooth but noticeable shifts

What problems does each type tend to show?

These are typical signs, not a diagnosis. The same symptom can have different causes, which is why Jacky tests the gearbox before quoting.

Conventional automatic

  • Harsh, late or slipping gear changes
  • A delay when you select Drive or Reverse
  • Shudder at steady speed, which can point to the torque converter

CVT

  • A whining or humming noise that rises with speed
  • Engine revs rising without the car speeding up as it should
  • Shudder or jerking when pulling away

Dual-clutch

  • Juddering or jerking when moving off slowly, for example in a car park or traffic queue
  • Hesitation before the car moves
  • A gearbox warning on the dashboard, sometimes from the control unit

If you notice any of these, see gearbox slipping, car jerks when changing gear or gearbox noise.

Why does the gearbox type matter for repair and servicing?

  • Fluid. Each type uses its own fluid. The wrong fluid can damage the gearbox, so always use the type your gearbox maker specifies. More in our ATF guide.
  • Diagnosis. The tests and fault codes differ between the three designs, so the gearbox should be tested before any parts are changed.
  • Cost. A conventional automatic, a CVT and a dual-clutch gearbox are built differently, so the repair options and costs differ too. Read gearbox overhaul vs replacement for the choices.

Questions

Is a CVT less reliable than a normal automatic?
Not by itself. How long any gearbox lasts depends a lot on the right fluid, regular checks and how the car is driven. Follow your car maker's service schedule for your gearbox type.
Is DSG the same as a dual-clutch gearbox?
Yes. DSG is the name the Volkswagen group uses for its dual-clutch gearboxes. Other makers use other names for the same idea.
Can I use the same gearbox oil for any automatic?
No. Conventional automatics, CVTs and dual-clutch gearboxes each need their own specified fluid. Using the wrong one can cause shifting problems or damage.
