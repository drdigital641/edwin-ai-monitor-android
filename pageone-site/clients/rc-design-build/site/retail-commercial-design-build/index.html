Design & Build for Retail and Commercial Spaces

Design and build brings planning, design and project coordination under one point of contact, from your first brief to handover. It suits Singapore shop owners, office and showroom tenants, and cafe or salon operators who would rather manage one conversation than coordinate several separate contractors themselves.

What does design and build mean for a commercial space?

Design and build means one party coordinates the planning, the design and the delivery of your fit-out, instead of you hiring a designer, then separate trades, then chasing each of them. The aim is a single line of communication from brief to handover.

For a retail or commercial unit, this usually covers understanding how the space will be used, drawing a layout, choosing materials, sequencing the trades and keeping the project moving once work starts on site.

It does not remove your decisions. You still approve the layout, finishes and scope. What changes is that the people planning the work are also thinking about how it will be built.

Who design and build suits

This approach tends to suit business owners who are opening or refreshing a space and do not want to run the project day to day.

  • First-time tenants fitting out a bare or previously used unit.
  • Shop owners changing layout, rebranding or adding a counter, shelving and lighting together.
  • Offices and showrooms needing reception, display and meeting areas to work as one design.
  • Cafe and salon operators who need counters, seating, storage and finishes planned around daily operations.
  • Owners with no time to compare several quotations for trades that depend on each other.

Problems it addresses include trades that clash on site, drawings that do not match what is built, and scope gaps that appear only after work begins.

Design choices and trade-offs to discuss early

Design decisions made early are cheaper to change than those made mid-build. A good planning stage puts the main trade-offs in front of you while they are still easy to adjust.

  • Open layout or defined zones: open plans feel larger but offer less storage and less separation of back-of-house areas.
  • Fixed or flexible fixtures: built-in joinery looks cohesive; freestanding units are easier to move when the layout changes.
  • Durable or decorative finishes: high-traffic zones often need tougher surfaces than feature areas.
  • Lighting and power positions: deciding where displays and counters sit before electrical routing avoids rework.
  • Lease length: a short lease may favour simpler, easily reinstated finishes.

What drives the budget in a design and build project?

Budget is driven mainly by the size of the space, the number of trades involved, the complexity of the joinery and finishes, and how much existing work must be removed or reinstated. Design effort itself depends on how clear your brief is.

Other drivers worth discussing early:

  • Custom-made items versus standard components.
  • Material grade and finish choices.
  • Amount of electrical and lighting work.
  • Restrictions on working hours or delivery access.
  • Changes after scope is agreed.

A clear scope and a realistic allowance for changes keep the budget conversation honest. No figures are given here because they depend entirely on your unit and requirements.

Planning and lead-time factors

Timing depends on factors that are mostly known only after a site assessment. Rather than promise dates, a planned project identifies what could affect the programme and flags it early.

  • How long design decisions and approvals take on your side.
  • Whether custom items need to be made before installation.
  • Material availability and delivery arrangements.
  • Landlord or mall requirements for starting work.
  • How trades overlap and what must be finished before the next begins.

If you have a target opening date, share it at the start. Working backwards from it shows which decisions need to be made first.

Coordination, site access and approvals

Coordination is where design and build earns its place. Trades need access in a sensible order, deliveries need loading arrangements, and shared spaces need protection.

Check with your landlord or mall management about approvals, permitted working hours and contractor rules. Some works may need submissions by qualified professionals; these should be identified at the site assessment stage.

A planned project keeps a single list of open items, who is responsible for each, and what is waiting on whom. That list is what stops small issues becoming delays.

Common mistakes design and build helps avoid

Most fit-out problems are not dramatic. They are small gaps between trades that nobody owned. Planning the project as a whole is meant to catch them early.

  • Joinery ordered before power and data positions are fixed.
  • Flooring laid before heavy fixtures and deliveries are finished.
  • Lighting chosen after the ceiling is closed.
  • Landlord or mall requirements discovered after work has started.
  • Scope descriptions that differ between quotations, making them hard to compare.

The intended process and what to prepare

An R&C project is planned to follow these steps:

  1. Discuss: your business, how the space is used and what must change.
  2. Assess: review the unit, constraints and existing conditions.
  3. Plan: layout options, materials and a defined scope for each trade.
  4. Coordinate: sequence the work and manage access and approvals.
  5. Execute and review: check progress against the scope.
  6. Hand over: final walk-through and a list of items to close out.

To start, send us your floor plan, photos of the unit, what the space will be used for and your target opening date. We'll discuss your scope and the next step.

Frequently asked questions

Is design and build different from hiring a designer and contractor separately?

In a separate arrangement you coordinate the designer and each trade yourself. Design and build places planning and coordination under one point of contact, so the people drawing the plan also consider how it will be built. You still approve the layout, finishes and scope at each stage.

Can I use design and build for a small shop?

Yes. Small units often benefit because limited space means carpentry, electrical and finishes must fit together closely. The planning effort is scaled to the unit, and you can choose which parts of the scope you want coordinated rather than taking everything.

Do I need drawings before I contact you?

No. A rough floor plan, a few photos of the unit and a description of how you will use it are enough to start a conversation. Drawings and layout options are developed during planning, based on your brief and the site conditions.

Does design and build include audio, screens and feature lighting?

Those are scoped separately under the audio, visual and lighting pages, because they depend on how you want the space to feel. They can be planned alongside the main fit-out so power, ceiling and wall provisions are considered early.

Who handles landlord or mall approvals?

Approvals are generally between the tenant and the landlord or mall management. Planning can help identify what to ask and when, and some works may need submissions by qualified professionals, which should be identified at the site assessment stage.
