Cafes and counters

Cafe Counters, Seating and Service Areas

For cafes and service counters, the interior scope usually covers the ordering counter, display, seating, wall and floor finishes, and the lighting, music and menu screens that set the mood. This page suits operators planning customer-facing areas, and it does not cover kitchen or food-licensing matters.

What does the customer-facing side of a cafe need?

A cafe needs a counter that keeps orders moving, display that tempts customers, seating that fits the space, and finishes that tolerate spills, cleaning and heavy traffic. Atmosphere then comes from lighting, sound and a consistent material palette that suits your brand and your customers.

The same applies to dessert counters, juice bars, bakery shop fronts and other service counters. This page covers the front-of-house interior only: counters, seating, display and finishes.

Kitchen layout, equipment, ventilation and food-licensing requirements are the operator's own matter and are not part of what is offered here. Check them separately with the relevant parties.

Typical scope mix

Customer-facing cafe projects usually include several of these items:

  • Counter and back-bar joinery: service counter, display cabinet, shelves and cup or packaging storage. See commercial carpentry.
  • Seating joinery: built-in benches, banquettes, wall shelves and tops for tables.
  • Walls and ceilings: feature panelling, paint finishes and ceiling details.
  • Flooring: a hard-wearing, easy-to-clean surface for the seating area.
  • Electrical: power at the counter, wall sockets for customers and general lighting.
  • AVL: menu screens, discreet ceiling speakers and mood lighting.

Counter and seating layout

These points are typical starting places to confirm on site and with your own staff and equipment.

  • Counter height: many service counters sit around 900-1,050 mm high; confirm with your staff and equipment.
  • Queue space: leave room in front of the counter so a queue does not block seating or the door.
  • Staff movement: plan the sequence of taking orders, payment, preparation and handover so staff are not crossing each other.
  • Display: place cabinets so customers can see items as they queue, with good lighting inside.
  • Seating mix: a blend of bench, bar and table seating can use awkward corners and edges well, and built-in benches save floor space.
  • Pick-up point: a separate end of the counter helps delivery riders and takeaway customers.

The guide to planning a custom shop counter applies here too.

Materials that cope with cafe use

Cafe surfaces face spills, hot cups, frequent wiping and constant contact. Choose materials for cleaning and wear first, then for look.

  • Counter tops: solid surface, laminate and sealed timber are all used; each has different heat, scratch and stain behaviour, so check how the supplier recommends caring for it.
  • Counter fronts: panels near the floor take scuffs and mop splashes, so consider moisture-resistant or easily replaceable finishes.
  • Floors: slip behaviour and ease of cleaning matter as much as appearance.
  • Walls: washable paint at seat height prevents marks from chair backs and bags.
  • Seating: fixed benches need sturdy backing and cleanable upholstery; loose chairs are easier to replace.

Ask any supplier how a finish should be cleaned, and avoid products that stain or mark easily where customers sit and lean.

Music, menu screens and mood lighting

Sound sets the pace of a cafe. A set of small ceiling speakers spreads music evenly at a low level, avoiding loud corners near the counter. Volume zones can differ between seating and the ordering area.

Menu screens are easy to update for new items and promotions. They need power and cable routes planned behind the counter and a sensible viewing height. See display and visual systems.

Warm, layered lighting makes food and drinks look appealing and makes the room comfortable. Pendants over the counter, concealed lighting in display cabinets and dimmable general lighting are common ideas.

Think about the whole day. A bright, clear scene suits busy mornings, while softer settings suit evenings. Simple controls let staff change the mood without calling anyone in.

What drives budget and timing

Key drivers include the counter length and complexity, the number of built-in seats, the choice of top and front materials, the amount of electrical and data points needed, and the number of screens and speakers. Condition of the existing floor and ceiling also matters.

Programmes depend on materials, approvals and access. Mall units often have specific delivery and working-hour rules, and cafes frequently have a fixed opening date, so share that early.

Check with your landlord or mall management about approvals, permitted working hours and contractor rules.

What to prepare

An R&C project is planned to follow a conversation about your scope, a site assessment, a layout proposal and then coordinated works. Some works may need submissions by qualified professionals; these should be identified at the site assessment stage.

  1. Floor plan and unit photos.
  2. A list of the equipment that will sit on or behind the counter, with sizes.
  3. Menu type and display needs, such as chilled or ambient cabinets.
  4. Seat count you hope to fit.
  5. Brand mood references and target opening date.

Wider coordination sits under design and build.

Frequently asked questions

Do you handle kitchen layouts or exhaust systems?

No. The scope on this page is front-of-house: counters, seating, display and finishes. Kitchen layout, equipment, ventilation and food-licensing requirements are the operator's own matter and should be arranged separately with the relevant parties.

Can a counter be designed around my equipment?

Yes. Share the dimensions of machines, display units and the till, and the counter can be planned with the right openings, shelves and cable routes. Equipment weight, ventilation space and service access are worth confirming early.

What counter top suits a busy cafe?

It depends on heat, stains, scratching and the look you want. Solid surface, laminate and sealed timber each behave differently. Discuss your daily use so the top and its care routine match the way staff work.

Can menu screens be built into the wall behind the counter?

Usually yes. Screens can be mounted flush or on brackets with concealed wiring, provided power and data points are planned before finishes go up. Viewing distance and glare from windows are worth checking on site.

Can I renovate while my cafe is open?

It depends on the noise, dust and access involved. Some small works may be scheduled outside trading hours, but this must fit landlord and mall rules. A site assessment helps judge what is practical.
