Offices and showrooms

Office and Showroom Fit-Out in Singapore

Office and showroom fit-outs focus on how visitors first meet the space: a reception counter, a feature wall, meeting areas, display zones and the lighting and screens that tie them together. This page suits business owners and tenants updating a workplace or presenting products to customers.

What do offices and showrooms need from their interiors?

Offices and showrooms need a clear arrival point, comfortable working or browsing zones, meeting or consultation space and storage. Showrooms add the need to present products well. Both rely on first impressions, so reception and display areas often get the most design attention, followed by lighting and sound.

The two types overlap. A consultancy may want a polished reception and meeting room, while a product showroom needs flexible display bays, strong lighting and a screen to show catalogue or demonstration content.

Typical scope mix

Workplace projects usually involve several services working in order. Common elements include:

  • Reception joinery: a counter, back wall, waiting seating zone and brand signage panel. See commercial carpentry.
  • Partitions and ceilings: meeting rooms, pantry edges, and ceiling work that hides services, covered under commercial renovation.
  • Storage and shelving: built-in cabinets, display plinths and document storage.
  • Electrical: floor or wall power for desks, meeting tables and display units.
  • Floor and wall finishes: carpet tiles, hard flooring, feature paint or panelling.
  • AVL: meeting room displays, background sound, and lighting that highlights the reception or products.

Layout considerations

These are typical starting points to confirm on site and with your staff.

  • Reception: place the counter where visitors see it on entering, with room to wait without blocking the door. Include concealed space for a computer, cables and everyday stationery.
  • Circulation: keep routes from reception to meeting rooms short and obvious.
  • Meeting rooms: think about table size, screen position and how sound carries into open areas.
  • Showroom bays: allow space to walk around larger products, and flexibility to rearrange as stock changes.
  • Accessibility: consider wheelchair access, turning space and counter heights for visitors who need them.

Finishes that cope with workplace and showroom use

Reception counters and showroom plinths are touched, leaned on and bumped by trolleys. Hard-wearing surfaces help them keep their look.

Laminate, veneer and solid-surface tops are common counter choices, each with a different feel and upkeep. Timber feature elements, such as an oak front panel, add warmth, while painted or panelled walls in a calm colour such as olive green can frame the brand without overwhelming the room.

Flooring should suit foot traffic and any rolling loads, such as display trolleys or office chairs. Acoustic considerations also matter: soft finishes, ceiling treatment or wall panels can reduce echo in meeting rooms and open areas.

Audio, visual and lighting for these spaces

AVL is often where an office or showroom stands apart. A wall display in reception can show a welcome message or brand content. Meeting rooms may need screens, a sound solution and simple controls that staff can use without help.

Showrooms benefit from feature lighting aimed at products and a display or signage system that is easy to update. Background sound, if used, should stay low enough for conversation.

Plan cable routes, power and mounting points before joinery and finishes are fixed, so equipment sits neatly.

Simple control matters too. If staff need to turn on a meeting room display, adjust the lighting or change the music, a clear, labelled control is more useful than a complicated system that nobody wants to touch.

Budget and timing drivers

The final shape of the budget depends on the unit, not a template. Common drivers include:

  • Floor area and how much layout change is needed.
  • Custom joinery compared with standard furniture.
  • The number and size of screens, speakers and lighting zones.
  • Condition of existing ceilings, wiring and floors.
  • Whether works must happen outside normal hours.
  • Delivery times for specialist materials and equipment.

Check with your landlord or building management about approvals, permitted working hours and contractor rules. Offices in multi-tenant buildings often have restrictions on deliveries and noisy work.

What to prepare

An R&C project is planned around a conversation about your scope, a site assessment, a proposal, then coordinated works. Some works may need submissions by qualified professionals; these should be identified at the site assessment stage.

Useful items to bring:

  • A floor plan or measurements.
  • Headcount, meeting room needs and any equipment to be housed.
  • A product list and display needs, for showrooms.
  • Brand colours, logo files and sample references.
  • Your intended move-in or opening date.

For the wider approach, see design and build.

Frequently asked questions

Can you update only the reception area?

A reception-only update is a common scope. It can include a new counter, feature wall, lighting and a screen without disturbing the rest of the office. A site assessment helps confirm what can be done while staff remain at work.

What is the difference between an office and a showroom fit-out?

Offices focus on workflow, meeting spaces and storage, while showrooms add product display, lighting and visitor flow. Many businesses combine both, such as a reception that doubles as a small display area, so the scope mix is planned around your actual use.

Can screens and speakers be built into the joinery?

Often yes. Screens, wiring and speakers can be planned into feature walls, counters and ceilings so equipment looks tidy. This needs agreement early, because it affects carpentry sizes, power points and ventilation space for devices.

How do I keep noise down in meeting rooms?

Soft finishes, ceiling treatment, wall panels and door seals can all reduce sound travel. How much is needed depends on the room, the neighbouring spaces and how you use it, which a site visit helps to judge.

Do I need landlord approval for office works?

Many buildings have fit-out rules, so check with your landlord or building management about approvals, permitted working hours and contractor requirements before fixing a schedule. Some works may need submissions by qualified professionals, which should be identified at the site assessment stage.
