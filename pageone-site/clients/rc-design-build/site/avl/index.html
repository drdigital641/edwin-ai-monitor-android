AVL overview

Audio, Visual and Lighting for Commercial Spaces

AVL means audio, visual and lighting: the speakers, screens and feature lights that shape how a space feels. For shops, cafes, offices and showrooms, these work well when planned together with the fit-out, so cable paths, ceiling space, power and controls are settled before walls are closed and finishes go on.

What does AVL cover in a commercial space?

AVL covers the parts of a business interior that people hear and see rather than touch: background music and paging, displays and signage, and feature lighting with its controls. Together they set the mood, guide attention, make information easy to take in and shape how long customers want to stay.

AVL is a planning layer that sits across the other trades. It is separate from carpentry, tiling or general wiring, but it depends on all of them.

  • Audio: speakers, amplification, zoning and volume control.
  • Visual: displays, digital signage, menu boards and presentation screens.
  • Lighting: feature, accent and display lighting, with dimming and scenes.
  • Control: how staff switch, adjust and schedule all of the above.

Who AVL planning suits

AVL planning suits any business where atmosphere or information matters to the customer visit. The needs differ by space type, so the starting questions differ too.

  • Shops and boutiques: music that suits the brand, a window display that reads from outside, and lighting that shows products accurately.
  • Cafes and service counters: music zones for seating and queue areas, and menu boards that are easy to update.
  • Offices and meeting rooms: clear audio for calls and a screen that everyone around the table can see.
  • Showrooms: feature lighting on key pieces and screens that explain products without crowding the floor.

Why plan AVL during fit-out rather than after?

Cables, power points, ceiling space and control locations are far easier to arrange while walls, ceilings and joinery are still open. Adding equipment after handover often means surface trunking, visible cables or repeat work on finished surfaces, so planning early usually keeps the result tidier and avoids disturbing completed finishes.

The practical items to settle early are:

  • Conduit or cable paths from the equipment point to a central rack or cupboard.
  • Ceiling void depth and access panels where speakers or lights will sit.
  • Power outlets behind screens, in counters and near racks.
  • Blocking or backing in walls so screens can be mounted securely.
  • Space in carpentry for amplifiers, players and ventilation.

Check with your landlord or mall management about approvals, permitted working hours and contractor rules before any work starts.

How sound, screens and light work together

Each element affects the others. A bright screen next to a dim, warm accent light can look washed out or harsh. A loud music zone can undermine a quiet consultation corner. Planning them together avoids these clashes.

  • Brightness balance: screens, signage and display lights should not compete for attention.
  • Zoning: sound and light can follow the same zones, such as entrance, display floor, counter and back-of-house.
  • Scenes: one control can set a morning, peak-hour or closing mood across music and lighting.
  • Reflections: glossy finishes and glass change how screens and lights look, so finishes should be reviewed alongside equipment.

Scope: typically included and usually separate

AVL scope is easiest to manage when it is clearly divided from the other trades. A typical split looks like this, to be confirmed for each project.

Usually in AVLUsually separate
Speakers, amplifiers, zoningPower points and general wiring
Displays, mounts, signage playersCounters and display joinery
Feature and accent fittings, dimmingGeneral ceiling lighting circuits
Control panels and scenesPainting and wall finishes

General wiring and lighting installation sits under commercial electrical works, and custom housings for equipment sit under commercial carpentry.

What drives AVL cost and timing

No figure is useful before the space and the brief are known. These factors usually move the budget and the schedule most:

  • Number of zones, screens and lighting scenes.
  • Ceiling type and how easily cables can be routed.
  • Screen size, brightness needs and mounting method.
  • Whether content is updated by staff or by an outside provider.
  • Equipment availability and lead times for selected items.
  • Whether work happens during fit-out or in a finished unit with limited working hours.

Late changes to layout or finishes can also affect cable routes and fixing points, so settle the plan before ordering.

How an AVL project is planned to run

Each project is planned to follow these steps, with AVL discussed alongside the rest of the fit-out.

  1. Share your floor plan, unit photos and how the space will be used.
  2. Agree what each area needs to sound, show and look like.
  3. Mark speaker, screen and light positions on the plan, with cable paths and power.
  4. Coordinate with carpentry, electrical and ceiling work.
  5. Install, set up controls and walk through the scenes with staff.

To prepare, gather your floor plan, a target opening date, your brand mood references and a list of what staff will operate day to day.

Common AVL mistakes to avoid

Most problems with sound, screens and lights come from decisions made too late, not from the equipment itself. These are the patterns worth watching for.

  • Ordering equipment before the layout is fixed: positions then have to follow the equipment instead of the other way round.
  • No cable route: leads end up surface-run along finished walls or ceilings.
  • No power where it is needed: screens, racks and amplifiers need outlets, sometimes inside joinery.
  • Controls nobody understands: if staff cannot change music, screens or light scenes easily, settings stay wrong.
  • Ignoring finishes: glass, gloss tiles and bare concrete change how sound and light behave.

A short written plan covering positions, cable paths, power and who controls what avoids most of these.

Frequently asked questions

What does AVL stand for?

AVL stands for audio, visual and lighting. In a commercial interior it refers to speakers and music, screens and signage, and feature lighting with its controls, treated as one planned layer rather than three separate purchases made at different times.

Can AVL be added after the fit-out is finished?

It can, but options are often narrower. Without planned cable paths, ceiling access and power, equipment may need surface trunking or work on finished walls. Planning during fit-out usually allows cleaner routing and fewer compromises, though a site assessment will show what is practical.

Is AVL the same as electrical works?

No. General wiring, power points and lighting installation belong to electrical works. AVL covers the sound, screens, feature lighting and controls that use that power. The two should be planned together so outlets, circuits and cable routes match the equipment.

Do I need all three parts of AVL?

No. Many small spaces only need one or two, such as background music and a feature light over a display. A short discussion of how customers and staff use the space helps decide which parts are worth including now and which can wait.

What should I send to start a conversation?

A floor plan or sketch, a few photos of the unit, a description of how the space will be used and your target opening date are enough to begin. Mood references for sound and lighting are helpful but not required.
