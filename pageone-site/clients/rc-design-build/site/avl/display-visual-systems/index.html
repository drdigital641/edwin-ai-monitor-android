Display systems

Display and Visual Systems for Commercial Spaces

Display and visual systems include screens, digital signage, menu boards, presentation displays and video walls. They suit shops, cafes, showrooms and offices that need to show products, menus or information clearly. Good results depend on mounting, viewing distance, brightness, content management and cabling being planned before the walls are finished.

Who display systems suit

Screens are useful where information changes often or where motion helps attract attention. They are not necessary for every business, and a poorly placed screen can distract rather than help.

  • Shops: window or in-store screens for promotions and brand visuals.
  • Cafes and counters: menu boards that staff can update without reprinting.
  • Showrooms: product information beside the item, or a larger screen as a feature.
  • Offices: presentation screens for meeting rooms and a welcome display at reception.

Which type of display suits which use?

The right display depends on where it sits and what it shows. Standard commercial displays suit most indoor uses, while very bright or large formats are chosen for specific needs such as shopfront visibility or feature walls. The table below gives typical starting points to confirm on site.

UseTypical choiceThink about
Menu boardLandscape or portrait screensText size, glare
ShopfrontHigh-brightness displaySunlight, heat
Meeting roomWall displaySeat distance
Feature wallVideo wallBezels, cost

A video wall tiles several displays into one large image. It makes a strong feature but adds cabling, processing and mounting work.

How far should viewers be from a screen?

Viewing distance decides screen size and text size. As a typical starting point to confirm on site, larger screens and bigger text are needed the further away viewers stand. A menu read at a queue needs larger text than product details read at arm’s length.

  • Mark where customers will actually stand or sit.
  • Check the angle: screens placed too high or too far to the side are harder to read.
  • Test sample content at planned text sizes on the actual wall if possible.

Eye level and comfortable viewing angles also matter for meeting rooms, where every seat needs a clear line of sight.

Brightness, glare and shopfront screens

Screens near glass shopfronts compete with daylight and reflections. A standard indoor display can look dim or washed out when sunlight reaches it, and the screen itself can run warm in strong light.

  • Consider where the sun and bright lights fall at different times of day.
  • Use brighter displays or position screens away from direct light where possible.
  • Review glossy finishes nearby that could reflect the screen or its light into customers’ eyes.
  • Keep brightness in balance with surrounding feature lighting.

Ventilation around the screen matters too, especially if it sits inside a window box or cabinet.

Mounting and cabling

A screen is only as secure as its fixing. Wall mounts need suitable backing, and ceiling or floor-standing options need a plan for weight and access. Cabling for power, video and network should be planned at the same time.

  • Arrange blocking or backing in walls before boards are closed.
  • Run cable paths to a central rack or concealed player, rather than leaving loose leads.
  • Place power outlets close to the screen position so no extension leads are visible.
  • Allow service access so a screen can be removed or replaced later.

Power points and wiring are covered under commercial electrical works.

How is signage content managed?

Content can be managed in several ways, from a simple USB or media player to a cloud-based system that updates many screens at once. The right choice depends on how often content changes, how many screens there are and who will handle updates day to day.

  • Local playback: simple and low-maintenance, but updates are manual.
  • Network or cloud management: remote updates and scheduling, with a need for reliable network and account management.
  • Staff-edited menus: templates that non-technical staff can change.

Decide early who creates the content, who approves it and who handles faults. A screen showing outdated information is worse than no screen.

Planning for upkeep and future changes

Screens run for long hours, so think about what happens after installation. A little planning now saves disruption later.

  • Leave access to the back of each screen for servicing and replacement.
  • Choose mounts that allow a different screen size later without drilling new holes.
  • Keep spare conduit or cable routes where a future screen might be added.
  • Record logins, schedules and content files in a place the owner controls.
  • Agree who looks after faults and how they are reported.

Rebranding or menu changes often mean new artwork at new sizes, so confirm the screen shape and resolution requirements before content is designed.

Scope, budget drivers and process

Display scope usually covers screens, mounts, media players, cabling to the rack and setup. Budget drivers include screen count and size, brightness needs, mounting complexity, content management choice and equipment lead times.

Each project is planned to follow these steps:

  1. Review the floor plan and where customers or staff will view screens.
  2. Agree screen type, size, mounting and content method.
  3. Coordinate backing, power and cable paths with carpentry and electrical trades.
  4. Install, set up content and walk staff through updates.

Check with your landlord or mall management about approvals, permitted working hours and contractor rules.

Please prepare your floor plan, wall photos, sample content and target opening date.

It also helps to say what each screen should achieve, whether that is selling a promotion, showing a menu or informing visitors, because the purpose decides size, position and how often content must change.

Frequently asked questions

Can staff update a menu board themselves?

Usually yes, if the system is set up with simple templates or a media player that accepts new files. The method should be agreed at the start, including who is responsible for edits, so changes do not depend on outside help for every small update.

Will a normal screen work near my shopfront window?

It may look dim in strong daylight, and reflections can make it hard to read. Higher-brightness displays or a position away from direct light are common solutions. Conditions vary by unit orientation, so check the position on site.

What is a video wall?

A video wall tiles several displays into one large picture, often used as a feature behind a counter or in a showroom. It needs a robust mounting frame, careful alignment, extra cabling and content prepared for the combined size.

Do screens need special wall preparation?

Wall mounts need suitable backing or fixing points, especially for larger or heavier displays. This is easiest to arrange before boards are closed. A site assessment will show what the wall can take and whether extra support is needed.

How do I choose between local and cloud-managed content?

Local playback suits a single screen with infrequent changes. Cloud or network management suits several screens or frequent updates, but needs reliable internet and account management. Think about who will maintain content before choosing.
