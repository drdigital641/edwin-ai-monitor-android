Commercial audio

Commercial Audio Systems for Shops, Cafes and Offices

A commercial audio system delivers background music, announcements or meeting sound through speakers, an amplifier and controls. It suits shops, cafes, offices and showrooms that want even, comfortable sound at the right level in each area, with speaker positions and cable paths planned during fit-out rather than added afterwards.

Who commercial audio suits

Commercial audio suits businesses where sound is part of the customer experience or where people need to hear clearly. Typical cases include:

  • Boutiques and shops that want steady background music without hot spots near the door.
  • Cafes where seating, queue and counter areas need different levels.
  • Showrooms and salons where a calm consultation area sits beside a livelier display floor.
  • Offices that need clear sound in meeting rooms and simple announcements.

The common problem is uneven sound: loud near one speaker, thin at the back, or too present at the counter where staff must hear customers.

What goes into a background music system?

A background music system usually has a source, an amplifier or mixer, speakers and a way to control volume. The aim is even, comfortable coverage rather than loud sound, so several smaller speakers are often used instead of a few large ones, spread across the ceiling at a sensible spacing.

  • Source: a streaming player, media device or input from a staff device.
  • Amplification: sized to the number of speakers and zones.
  • Speakers: ceiling, wall or pendant types chosen for the room.
  • Controls: a wall panel or simple app so staff can adjust without hunting for equipment.

For more on choosing music for a shop floor, see the guide on background music for shops.

Ceiling, wall and other speaker choices

Speaker type affects both sound and appearance. The right choice depends on ceiling height, ceiling material and how much visible equipment the design can accept.

TypeSuitsTrade-off
CeilingEven coverage, clean lookNeeds void access and cable route
WallLow ceilings, feature wallsVisible; needs a secure fixing
PendantHigh or open ceilingsVisible; suspension points needed

Hard surfaces such as glass, stone and tile reflect sound, so a lively room may need softer finishes or more speakers at lower volume to stay comfortable.

How does zoning and volume control work?

Zoning splits a space into areas that each have their own volume, and sometimes their own source. A cafe might set the entrance, seating and counter separately, so staff at the counter can still hear orders while diners enjoy a gentler level further away. Each zone needs its own speaker group and control.

  • Group speakers by area on the plan before choosing equipment.
  • Decide who may change volume: staff at the counter, a manager only, or a locked preset.
  • Consider time-based presets, such as lower levels in the morning and higher in the evening.

Fewer zones are simpler to run. Add zones only where a real difference in use exists.

Paging and meeting-room audio basics

Paging sends spoken messages through the same speakers, either to the whole space or to selected zones. It is useful for back-of-house calls or general notices, and it needs a microphone input and a plan for how music should dip during an announcement.

Meeting rooms have different needs from retail floors:

  • Voices must be clear at every seat, so microphone and speaker positions matter more than volume.
  • Hard walls and glass can cause echo; soft furnishings or acoustic panels may help.
  • Display, microphone and speakers should connect simply so any visitor can start a call.

Screens for presentations are covered on the display and visual systems page.

Music use, planning and what drives the budget

Playing music to customers in a business may need permission from the rights holders. The owner should check what applies to their premises and the type of music they plan to play before launch.

Items that usually drive cost and timing:

  • Number of speakers and zones.
  • Ceiling type and ease of running cable.
  • Control method and number of control points.
  • Equipment availability for the chosen items.
  • Whether installation is during fit-out or in a finished, occupied unit with limited working hours.

Check with your landlord or mall management about approvals, permitted working hours and contractor rules.

Setting levels and keeping the system easy to run

A system that sounds right on the day of setup can drift if staff cannot control it. Agree simple rules early and note them down.

  • Set a sensible maximum volume per zone so music stays in the background.
  • Check levels at counter height and at seat height, with the shop empty and with customers present, since people absorb sound.
  • Keep the control panel where staff can reach it without leaving the counter.
  • Label inputs and zones in plain words, such as “seating” or “entrance”.
  • Keep a copy of the settings and the equipment list for future changes.

Where neighbours share walls or a mall shares common space, ask management about sound limits and quiet hours.

Scope, coordination and process

Audio scope usually includes speakers, amplifiers, controls and setup. Power points and wiring sit with commercial electrical works, and housings for equipment may be part of carpentry.

Each project is planned to follow these steps:

  1. Review the floor plan and how each area is used.
  2. Mark speaker zones, rack location and cable routes.
  3. Coordinate ceiling, power and joinery with other trades.
  4. Install, balance levels and walk staff through the controls.

Please prepare your floor plan, ceiling type if known, preferred control method and target opening date.

Also note any quiet areas, such as a consultation corner, and whether staff need to hear customers clearly at the counter. These details shape speaker positions more than the equipment list does.

Frequently asked questions

How many speakers does a small shop need?

It depends on ceiling height, shop shape and finishes. Smaller speakers placed evenly often sound more comfortable than a few large ones. A layout on your floor plan, reviewed at the site assessment, is the usual way to decide the number.

Can different areas play at different volumes?

Yes, through zoning. Speakers are grouped by area, such as entrance, seating and counter, and each group has its own level control. Zoning adds equipment and cabling, so it is sensible to reserve it for areas with a clear difference in use.

Do I need a licence to play music in my business?

Public music use in a business may need permission from rights holders. Rules depend on how and where music is played, so the owner should check what applies to their premises before launch. We do not advise on this.

Can speakers be hidden in the ceiling?

Often yes, if the ceiling has a void or a suitable cut-out position and a cable route can be provided. This is easiest to decide during fit-out. Open or exposed ceilings usually call for pendant or surface-mounted speakers instead.

What audio does a meeting room need?

At minimum, clear speakers, a microphone that picks up everyone at the table and a simple connection to the display. Echo from glass and hard walls often matters more than loudness, so room finishes are worth reviewing early.
