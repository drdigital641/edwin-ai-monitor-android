Feature lighting

Architectural and Feature Lighting for Commercial Interiors

Feature lighting highlights products, finishes and architecture beyond basic room lighting. It includes accent lights, display lighting and dimming scenes. It suits shops, showrooms, cafes and salons that want products and materials to look right, with colour temperature, colour rendering, glare and controls planned alongside the fit-out.

Who feature lighting suits

Feature lighting suits spaces where appearance affects what customers notice or buy. General lighting makes a room usable. Feature lighting gives it focus.

  • Shops: light on displays, shelves and the cash counter.
  • Showrooms: highlights on key pieces, materials and brand walls.
  • Cafes: softer light over seating and a clear, welcoming service counter.
  • Salons and studios: light that flatters faces at stations while keeping colour work accurate.

A common problem is flat, even lighting that makes products blend into the background, or fittings that create glare on glass and glossy finishes.

Feature, accent and display lighting: what is the difference?

These terms describe different jobs. Feature lighting draws attention to architecture such as a curved wall or ceiling detail. Accent lighting picks out specific items such as a product or sign. Display lighting lights shelves or cases so products can be seen clearly.

  • Feature: cove, wall wash, linear or sculptural fittings that shape the space.
  • Accent: adjustable spots or track lights aimed at a product or sign.
  • Display: strip or integrated lights inside shelves, cabinets and cases.

Integrated display lights need coordination with commercial carpentry so channels, drivers and cable routes are built into the joinery. The guide on lighting a retail display goes further into layout.

What colour temperature and colour rendering suit a space?

Colour temperature describes whether light looks warm or cool, and colour rendering describes how faithfully it shows true colours. Warmer light feels relaxed, cooler light feels crisp, and good rendering matters wherever customers judge fabric, food, skin tone or finishes.

  • Many shops and cafes use warmer tones for a welcoming feel, while offices and clinics-style spaces often use neutral tones. Confirm the mood with samples.
  • Keep tones consistent across a space, so fittings do not look mismatched.
  • Choose better colour rendering where colour accuracy matters, such as fashion, beauty or material showrooms.

Check samples against your actual finishes, as paint, timber and stone all react differently to light.

Controlling glare and reflections

Glare happens when a bright source is visible or reflects into the eye. It tires customers and makes displays harder to see. It is controlled by fitting choice, aiming angle and the surfaces around the light.

  • Use fittings with shielding or recessed positions so the bright source is not directly visible.
  • Aim accent lights at angles that avoid reflecting off glass cases and glossy tiles.
  • Check sight lines from the entrance and from seated positions, not just from standing height.
  • Think about how screens and signage sit within the lighting layout.

How do dimming and scene control work?

Dimming adjusts how bright a light is, and a scene stores a set of levels for several lights at once. Staff can then switch the space from opening mode to evening mode with one press instead of adjusting each circuit separately, which keeps the look consistent from day to day.

  • Choose dimmable fittings and compatible drivers from the start.
  • Group lights by purpose, such as display, ambient and signage, so scenes make sense.
  • Pick a control panel that staff can understand at a glance.
  • Consider time-based presets for opening and closing.

Controls can link with music, so one scene sets both light and sound. See commercial audio systems for zoning.

Where feature lighting ends and electrical works begin

Feature lighting covers the selection, aiming, dimming and control of decorative and display fittings. General lighting installation, circuits, distribution boards and power points belong to commercial electrical works.

The two must be planned together. Circuits need to match the fittings and dimming method, and ceiling positions should be agreed before ceiling work closes.

Some works may need submissions by qualified professionals; these should be identified at the site assessment stage.

Budget drivers, planning and process

Cost and schedule are shaped by the number of fittings, their type, ceiling complexity, dimming method, control system and equipment availability. Late layout changes affect where fittings and cables sit.

Each project is planned to follow these steps:

  1. Review the floor plan, fixtures and finishes.
  2. Agree zones, mood and scenes with the owner.
  3. Mark fittings and control points on the plan, and coordinate with carpentry and ceiling work.
  4. Install, aim and set scenes, then review with staff.

Check with your landlord or mall management about approvals, permitted working hours and contractor rules. Please prepare your floor plan, ceiling details, finish samples and target opening date.

Photos of the products or items to be lit are also useful. Bring examples of lighting you like from other spaces, and note what you dislike, such as harsh overhead light or visible fittings, so that the mood can be discussed before fittings are chosen.

If shelving or counters are being built, share the drawings early so that channels and driver space for lighting can be included in the joinery.

Frequently asked questions

What colour temperature should I choose for a shop?

There is no single answer. Warmer tones feel inviting and neutral tones feel crisp. Choose based on your brand mood and the products you sell, then test samples against your real finishes before committing to a full order.

Why do my products look different under shop lights?

Light sources vary in how well they show colour. Poor colour rendering, mixed colour temperatures or strong reflections from nearby finishes can all shift how products look. A sample test on site helps identify the cause before the full lighting is fixed.

Can lights be built into shelves and counters?

Yes, integrated display lighting is common in shelving, cabinets and counters. It needs channels, driver space and cable routes planned with the joinery, so it should be agreed before carpentry drawings are finalised.

Do I need dimming?

Not always, but it is useful if the space changes mood across the day or if display and ambient light need to be balanced. Dimming has to be specified from the start because fittings and drivers must be compatible with the control method.

Is feature lighting part of electrical works?

Partly. General lighting installation and power sit under electrical works, while feature lighting focuses on the choice, aiming and control of decorative and display fittings. The two scopes are planned together so circuits match the fittings.
