Commercial renovation

Commercial Renovation and Fit-Out for Shops and Offices

Commercial renovation covers the fit-out or refurbishment of a business unit: removing non-structural items, building partitions and ceilings, making good surfaces and coordinating the trades on site. It suits Singapore shop owners and tenants preparing a new unit, refreshing an old one or reinstating a space at lease end.

Who commercial renovation suits

Renovation is the on-site execution of a fit-out or refurbishment. It suits businesses whose space needs physical change before it can trade.

  • Tenants taking over a bare, shell or previously fitted unit.
  • Shop owners doing a small shop renovation to change layout or refresh the look.
  • Offices and showrooms reconfiguring meeting rooms, reception and open areas.
  • Cafe and salon operators adapting an existing unit to their service flow.
  • Tenants approaching the end of a lease who need reinstatement.

Common problems include outdated layouts, worn finishes, inefficient use of space and uncertainty about what the unit must look like when you leave.

Because renovation touches almost every other trade, it is often where the overall sequence of a project is set. Clear scope and early site checks save more effort here than anywhere else.

What does a fit-out or refurbishment scope usually cover?

A fit-out scope usually covers removing unwanted non-structural items, building new partitions and ceilings, preparing walls and floors, making good, and coordinating the finishing trades. Exact inclusions are listed for each project, so nothing is left to assumption and every party knows who is responsible for what.

Partitions, ceilings and demolition: general points

Removal work should be limited to non-structural elements such as old fixtures, finishes and light partitions. Anything that might be structural, or that affects services running through the unit, should be identified at the site assessment stage and handled by the right professionals.

  • Partitions: light framed partitions suit most commercial layouts; consider sound separation where privacy matters.
  • Ceilings: false ceilings can hide services and house lighting, but they reduce height and need access for maintenance.
  • Making good: patching walls and floors after removal sets the quality of every finish that follows.

Never assume a wall is non-structural. Confirm before any removal is planned.

Layout and finish trade-offs

Renovation choices usually balance appearance, durability, cost and how long you intend to stay in the unit.

  • Full refurbishment or targeted refresh: a refresh to flooring, paint and fixtures can change a unit without rebuilding it.
  • Permanent or reinstatable work: short leases often favour simple, removable finishes.
  • Open or compartmentalised: open areas feel larger but offer less storage and separation.
  • Ceiling height or concealed services: hiding services looks cleaner but reduces height.

What drives the renovation budget?

Renovation cost is driven by the size of the unit, how much needs removing, the number of trades, the finish quality and how difficult access and working restrictions make the site. Hidden conditions discovered after removal can also change scope.

  • Condition of the existing walls, floors and ceilings.
  • Extent of new partitions, ceilings and joinery.
  • Quality of finishes and fixtures.
  • Restricted working hours or limited loading access.
  • Late design changes and rework.

No figures are quoted on this page because every unit is different. A clear scope document is the strongest protection against surprises. Allowing a margin for unknowns is sensible.

Planning and lead-time factors

Renovation duration depends on the scope, the sequence of trades and what happens on site once the unit is opened up. No timeline is promised here.

  • Time to finalise layout and finish choices.
  • Landlord or mall requirements before work can begin.
  • Items that must be made or ordered ahead, such as joinery and special finishes.
  • Order of trades: ceilings and services before flooring and painting.
  • Curing or drying needs of some materials.

Work back from your target opening date to identify what must be decided first.

Coordination, site access and lease-end reinstatement

Check with your landlord or mall management about approvals, permitted working hours and contractor rules. Some works may need submissions by qualified professionals; these should be identified at the site assessment stage.

Site protection matters in shared buildings: lifts, corridors and neighbouring units need protecting from dust and noise. Waste removal and material delivery should be arranged around building rules.

Reinstatement returns the unit to the condition your lease requires. Read the lease early, record the unit's condition before work starts, and plan fit-out choices with removal in mind. Reinstatement scope depends entirely on your lease terms.

Common renovation mistakes to avoid

Renovation projects go off course for ordinary reasons, most of which can be reduced with a clear scope and early checks.

  • Starting work before landlord or mall requirements are understood.
  • Assuming a wall can be removed without confirming what it is.
  • Choosing finishes without thinking about lease-end reinstatement.
  • Leaving electrical and lighting positions undecided until ceilings are closed.
  • Comparing quotations that cover different scopes.
  • Making many design changes once work is under way.

Keep a written record of agreed changes, and photograph the unit before work starts so the starting condition is clear to everyone.

The intended process and what to prepare

Each renovation project is planned to follow these steps:

  1. Discuss your intended use and any constraints.
  2. Assess the unit on site.
  3. Define scope, layout and finishes.
  4. Sequence the trades and arrange access.
  5. Execute, with progress checks against scope.
  6. Walk through, close out items and hand over.

Send us your floor plan, unit photos, relevant lease notes and your target opening date, and we'll discuss your scope. Renovation is usually planned alongside painting and finishing, tiling and flooring and design and build coordination.

Frequently asked questions

How is a small shop renovation different from a full fit-out?

A small shop renovation usually changes part of the unit, such as fixtures, flooring, paint and lighting, while keeping the basic layout. A full fit-out builds out the space from a bare or heavily altered state. The right scope depends on the unit condition and your plans.

Do you handle structural changes?

Renovation scope here covers non-structural items only. If a proposed change might affect structure or building services, it should be identified at the site assessment stage and handled by suitably qualified professionals before any work is planned.

What does reinstatement at lease end involve?

Reinstatement returns the unit to the condition your lease specifies, which may include removing fixtures, making good surfaces and repainting. What is required depends on your tenancy agreement, so check it early and plan your fit-out accordingly.

Can I stay open during renovation?

It depends on the scope, the building rules and how disruptive the work is. Noisy or dusty work usually makes trading difficult, so many businesses close the unit, or part of it. Discuss your trading needs during planning.

What should I prepare before asking for a renovation plan?

Gather your floor plan, photos of the unit, your intended use, lease notes about alterations and reinstatement, and your target opening date. Even partial information is enough to start the discussion, and gaps can be filled at the site assessment.
