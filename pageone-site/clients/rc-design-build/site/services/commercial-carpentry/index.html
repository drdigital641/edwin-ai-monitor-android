Commercial carpentry

Commercial Carpentry for Shops and Business Premises

Commercial carpentry covers custom-built counters, cabinets, shelving, storage, display fixtures and reception joinery for business premises. It suits Singapore retail shops, offices, showrooms, cafes and salons that need fixtures sized to their products, equipment and layout rather than standard furniture that almost fits.

Who commercial carpentry suits

Custom carpentry makes sense when off-the-shelf furniture wastes space, does not suit your stock, or looks out of place in the interior you want.

  • Retail shops needing display shelving, wall units, cash counters and fitting areas.
  • Offices and showrooms needing reception counters, feature walls and meeting-room storage.
  • Cafes and service counters needing front-of-house counters, display and back-bar storage.
  • Salons and beauty studios needing stations, product storage and waiting-area joinery.

Typical problems solved: awkward corners, mismatched fixtures, not enough storage and counters that do not fit equipment such as a POS terminal or a coffee machine.

What does a commercial carpentry scope usually include?

A commercial carpentry scope usually covers measuring on site, producing drawings for approval, making or sourcing the joinery, delivering it and installing it. Surface finishes, hardware and fixings are normally specified as part of each item, so the final result matches the drawings you approved before anything is made.

Materials and finishes: options and trade-offs

The material you choose affects durability, appearance, cleaning and how well the joinery copes with a busy commercial environment.

  • Laminate: practical and wipe-clean, with wide colour and texture choice; edges can show wear if poorly detailed.
  • Timber veneer: a natural look, but needs a sealed finish and care around moisture.
  • Solid timber: warm and repairable, though more sensitive to changes in humidity.
  • Solid-surface or stone-look tops: good for counters; weight and joins need planning.
  • Metal and glass accents: useful for display, but fixings and cleaning need thought.

Choose tougher surfaces where customers lean, queue or place bags, and save delicate finishes for areas that see less handling. Our guide on retail display shelving materials goes into more detail.

Sizing and layout: starting points to confirm on site

Dimensions depend on the people and equipment using the fixture. Many service counters sit around 900-1,050 mm high, and many shelving units are designed with the top shelf within comfortable reach, but confirm with your staff, equipment and products before fixing sizes.

  • Leave clear walkways between fixtures, especially if customers carry bags or trolleys.
  • Check how doors and drawers open against neighbouring units.
  • Plan adjustable shelves if your stock changes often.
  • Consider an accessible counter section where customers may need it.
  • Allow ventilation and access for equipment kept inside cabinets.

For counters in particular, see planning a custom shop counter.

What drives the budget for custom joinery?

Budget is driven mainly by the quantity and complexity of items, material choice, finish quality, hardware and how difficult installation is. Fully custom pieces with curves, concealed lighting or integrated equipment need more design and making effort than simple boxes.

  • Number of separate fixtures and their sizes.
  • Material and finish selection.
  • Hardware such as hinges, drawer runners and locks.
  • Integrated features like cable routes and lighting channels.
  • Site access for delivery and installation.
  • Design changes after drawings are approved.

Planning and lead-time factors

Carpentry is often on the critical path, because fixtures are installed late but must be planned early. No timelines are promised here; instead, these factors usually shape the programme.

  • Time to agree drawings and finish samples.
  • Whether items are made off site before installation.
  • Readiness of walls, floors and electrical provisions at installation.
  • Delivery access, including lifts and loading areas.
  • Sequence with painting, flooring and lighting.

Confirm measurements on site after walls and floors are completed. Joinery made to drawings alone can clash with real wall conditions.

Coordination and site access

Joinery installation involves noise, dust and large items moving through shared areas. Check with your landlord or mall management about approvals, permitted working hours and contractor rules.

Carpentry must also be coordinated with other trades. Electrical points should be positioned before counters are built, flooring should be completed before heavy units are placed, and painting should be sequenced so that finished joinery is protected or touched up afterwards. A single coordinator, as described under design and build, helps keep that order clear.

Common carpentry mistakes to avoid

Most joinery problems come from decisions made too late or from measurements taken too early.

  • Building a counter before confirming equipment sizes and cable routes.
  • Choosing a delicate finish for a surface customers touch constantly.
  • Forgetting that doors and drawers need clearance to open.
  • Leaving no adjustability in shelving when stock will change.
  • Not asking how fixtures will be removed if the lease requires reinstatement.
  • Comparing quotations that list different materials, hardware or finishes.

A short written specification for each item, covering size, material, finish and hardware, makes quotations comparable and gives everyone the same reference at handover.

The intended process and what to prepare

An R&C carpentry project is planned to follow these steps:

  1. Discuss what you display, store and sell, and how staff and customers move.
  2. Measure the space and review site conditions.
  3. Develop drawings and finish options for your approval.
  4. Confirm the scope, then arrange making and installation.
  5. Inspect, adjust and hand over.

Frequently asked questions

Can you make a custom shop counter to fit my POS and equipment?

A custom counter can be planned around your POS terminal, printer, card reader and storage needs. Share equipment sizes and cable requirements early so cutouts, drawers and power access are designed in. Final dimensions should be confirmed on site before anything is made.

What is the difference between carpentry and renovation?

Carpentry covers made-to-fit fixtures such as counters, shelving and cabinets. Renovation covers the wider fit-out of the unit, including partitions, ceilings and making good. Many projects need both, and they are planned together so the order of work makes sense.

Do I need drawings before I enquire about retail display shelving?

No. A rough floor plan, photos of the space and a list of the products you want to display are enough to start. Drawings are prepared during planning so you can approve sizes, materials and layout before anything is made.

Which material suits a busy counter?

Hard-wearing laminate or solid-surface tops are often considered for busy counters because they wipe clean and resist daily handling. The right choice depends on appearance, cleaning routine and budget, so discuss samples and edge details before deciding.

Can carpentry be removed at the end of a lease?

Built-in joinery can often be dismantled, but how easily depends on how it was fixed. If your lease requires reinstatement, mention it early so fixtures can be designed with removal and making good in mind.
