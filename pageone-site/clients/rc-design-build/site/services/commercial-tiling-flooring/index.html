Tiling & flooring

Commercial Tiling and Flooring for Business Spaces

Commercial tiling and flooring covers floor preparation and the laying of tiles, vinyl or SPC planks, and epoxy-type coatings in business premises. It suits Singapore shops, offices, showrooms, cafes and salons that need floors suited to foot traffic, cleaning routines and the look of the space.

Who commercial tiling and flooring suits

Flooring is the surface customers see first and staff stand on all day. It suits anyone whose floor is worn, outdated, uneven or simply wrong for the use.

  • Retail shops wanting a durable, easily cleaned floor that suits the brand.
  • Offices and showrooms wanting a quieter, more polished surface.
  • Cafes and salons needing floors that handle spills, product and frequent cleaning.
  • Tenants reinstating a unit to meet lease terms.

Problems it addresses include cracked or lifting tiles, uneven surfaces, staining, and floors that look tired next to new joinery.

Flooring is also one of the harder things to change once the shop is trading, so it is worth deciding carefully at the start rather than planning to replace it later.

What does a flooring scope usually include?

A flooring scope usually covers checking the existing surface, removing old floor finishes if needed, preparing the base, laying the new finish and completing edges and transitions. Preparation often decides how well the final floor performs, so it should be described clearly in the scope rather than assumed.

Which floor type suits a commercial space?

No single floor suits every business. Tiles, vinyl or SPC planks and epoxy-type coatings each suit different traffic, cleaning and appearance needs. The right choice balances durability, slip behaviour, maintenance and how long you will stay in the unit, and is safest confirmed with samples and the actual site conditions in view.

  • Tiles: hard-wearing and varied in look; grout lines need cleaning and joints can crack if the base moves.
  • Vinyl or SPC: quicker to lay on a prepared floor, softer underfoot and often easier to replace; can mark under heavy point loads.
  • Epoxy-type coatings: seamless and easy to wipe, though preparation is critical and repairs can show.

Ask for samples and test them in your own lighting before committing.

Foot traffic, slip and cleaning trade-offs

Smooth, glossy floors look sharp but can be slippery when wet. Textured floors grip better but hold dirt and take longer to clean. Match the surface to where it is used.

  • Entrances and wet areas: favour surfaces with more grip, and consider mats at the door.
  • Counters and queues: expect concentrated wear and plan tougher finishes there.
  • Cleaning routine: consider what cleaning products and equipment your staff will use.
  • Noise and comfort: hard floors echo; softer finishes feel quieter.
  • Colour: very light floors show marks; very dark floors show dust.

Ask for slip-related information on any product you shortlist and discuss it with your supplier. Do not rely on appearance alone.

Floor preparation: why it matters

Preparation covers cleaning, levelling, repairing cracks and making sure the base is dry and sound. A new finish will show every flaw underneath, so skipping preparation usually leads to hollow tiles, ridges or peeling coatings.

  • Check levels across the unit and at doorways.
  • Allow for the thickness of the new finish where it meets existing floors.
  • Identify moisture issues before covering the surface.
  • Allow drying or curing before heavy use.

Preparation is where hidden conditions are most often found, so it deserves attention in the budget and programme.

What drives the flooring budget?

Flooring cost is driven mainly by floor area, the material selected, the condition of the existing floor, how much preparation is needed and the complexity of the layout, such as patterns and many edges. Access and working-hour restrictions also affect how efficiently the work can be carried out.

  • Material type and specification.
  • Removal and disposal of old finishes.
  • Amount of levelling or repair.
  • Tile size, pattern and cutting.
  • Access for moving heavy materials.
  • Working-hour restrictions.

No figures are given here because they depend on the unit and the floor you choose.

Planning, coordination and site access

Flooring has a sensitive place in the sequence. Dusty or heavy work should finish before the new floor is laid, and the floor needs protecting afterwards. Timing depends on scope, drying or curing needs, and delivery. No timeline is promised.

Check with your landlord or mall management about approvals, permitted working hours and contractor rules, especially for noisy removal and heavy material deliveries. Coordinate with carpentry so heavy units go in after the floor is ready, and with painting so skirting and walls are protected.

Common flooring mistakes to avoid

Floors rarely fail because of the material itself. They fail because of what was skipped beneath it or what was asked of it afterwards.

  • Laying a new finish over an unprepared or damp base.
  • Choosing a glossy surface for an entrance that gets wet.
  • Judging a sample under showroom light instead of your own.
  • Forgetting that heavy joinery and equipment will sit on the floor.
  • Not allowing for the finished floor height where it meets another floor.
  • Opening to customers before the finish has fully cured or dried.

Ask what care and cleaning products suit the finish, and give your cleaning staff those instructions at handover.

Frequently asked questions

Is vinyl or SPC better than tiles for a shop?

Neither is always better. Tiles are hard-wearing and varied in look, while vinyl or SPC is often quicker to lay and softer underfoot. The right choice depends on traffic, cleaning, base condition and how long you plan to stay. Review samples and discuss trade-offs.

Can new flooring go over my existing floor?

Sometimes, depending on the condition, level and type of the existing surface. It needs assessing on site. Laying over an unsound or uneven base usually causes problems later, so preparation or removal may be recommended.

Are epoxy-type floors suitable for retail or cafes?

They can be, where a seamless, wipe-clean surface suits the space. Preparation is critical and repairs can show, so discuss finish, grip and cleaning with your supplier. Decide based on your use rather than appearance alone.

How do I choose a floor that is not slippery?

Consider where the floor is used, whether it gets wet and how it will be cleaned. Ask suppliers for slip-related information on shortlisted products and match textured surfaces to entrances and wet areas. Appearance alone is not a reliable guide.

Do I need to remove my floor at lease end?

That depends on your lease terms. Some tenancies require the floor to be returned to its original condition. Read your agreement early and tell us, so flooring choices can consider removal and making good.
