Background Music for Shops

Good shop music is even, comfortable and easy for staff to control. Plan speaker placement for consistent coverage, split the space into zones where needed, set volume for conversation, choose a reliable source, and give staff simple controls. Also check the music licensing requirements for playing music in public.

Planning guide · 6 min read · Published 24 October 2026

What makes background music work in a shop?

Background music works when it is even across the space, comfortable at conversation level and matched to your brand. Customers should barely notice the system itself. Loud spots, quiet corners and distorted sound are signs that the speakers, levels or layout need rethinking.

  • Consistent level from the entrance to the back of the shop.
  • Clear, undistorted sound at low volume.
  • Music that suits your customers and the time of day.
  • Staff who can pause, change or lower it without trouble.

Think of audio as part of the shop atmosphere, alongside lighting and layout. It should support how people shop, not compete with the counter conversation.

Where should speakers go?

Place several smaller speakers spread across the ceiling or walls rather than one or two large ones. This delivers even coverage at lower volume, so people near a speaker are not overwhelmed. Position them to cover seating, browsing and queuing areas evenly.

  1. Mark the areas where customers and staff spend time.
  2. Check ceiling type and height, since they affect which speakers suit.
  3. Avoid placing speakers directly over the counter, where staff need to talk.
  4. Consider hard surfaces such as glass and tile, which reflect sound.
  5. Leave access for servicing and wiring.

Discreet ceiling speakers keep the look clean, while wall or surface-mounted units may suit shops with a limited ceiling void. Confirm the options during the site assessment.

Do I need separate audio zones?

You need zones if different parts of the premises need different volume or content. A fitting room, a back-of-house area, a seating section and a main floor often benefit from separate level control, so each can be adjusted without disturbing the others.

  • Entrance and main shop floor.
  • Seating or waiting areas, where conversation matters more.
  • Fitting rooms or treatment rooms, which may need quieter levels.
  • Staff-only areas, where music may not be wanted at all.

More zones mean more wiring, amplifier channels and controls, which affects cost. A small single-room shop may only need one zone with good speaker spacing.

How loud should shop music be?

Set the volume so staff and customers can talk comfortably at the counter without raising their voices. The right level varies with the business, the time of day and the noise from outside, so adjust on site rather than relying on a number from a specification.

  • Test with the shop empty and again with customers present.
  • Check near the entrance, the counter and the back of the shop.
  • Lower levels slightly in quieter periods and when staff are on calls.
  • Consider how sound travels to neighbouring units and corridors, and review the mall or landlord rules.

Set a sensible maximum level in the system so that staff cannot accidentally make the music too loud.

What music sources can I use?

Common sources include a streaming service intended for business use, a dedicated media player, a tablet or computer, or a radio feed. Choose one that is reliable and easy to restart, and check that its terms allow use in a shop.

  • Consumer apps may not be intended for commercial playback; check the terms.
  • Business music services usually offer scheduling and playlists.
  • Wired sources are generally more stable than wireless in busy buildings.
  • Consider a backup, such as a stored playlist, for when the connection fails.

Think about who chooses the music, how often it changes, and whether it needs to match different times of day or campaigns.

What should I check about music licensing?

Playing music in a shop is a public performance, so owners should check what licensing or permissions apply to the music they play. Do not assume a personal streaming subscription covers it. Ask your music provider or the relevant rights organisations what you need.

  • Ask your music provider whether their service is meant for business use.
  • Ask what additional permissions may apply to public playback.
  • Keep records of your subscriptions and any agreements.
  • Check your lease or mall rules for any restrictions on music or noise.

This guide does not give legal advice. Requirements can change, so confirm the current position yourself before you start playing music.

What should be planned before installation?

Plan cable routes, amplifier location, power and ceiling access before finishes go up. Audio is easiest to install neatly while ceilings and walls are still open, so raise it early in the fit-out schedule instead of after painting and joinery are complete.

  • A ventilated, accessible place for the amplifier and source equipment.
  • A power point near that equipment.
  • Cable routes through the ceiling or walls, avoiding clashes with lighting and air-conditioning.
  • Any joinery that needs openings for speakers or equipment.
  • Testing at low and normal volumes before handover.

Audio, electrical and carpentry scopes touch each other, so agree who handles each part. Power points and general wiring are usually electrical scope, while speakers, amplifiers and controls sit with audio.

How can staff control the system?

Give staff a few simple controls: volume, source selection, on and off, and perhaps zone levels. Fewer controls are easier to use. A wall panel, a labelled remote or a simple app can work, as long as everyone knows how to use it and where it is.

  • Keep one clear volume control within reach of the counter.
  • Label inputs in plain words, such as "Shop music".
  • Set maximum and minimum levels so music stays in a comfortable range.
  • Provide a short instruction card for new staff.

Frequently asked questions

How many speakers does a small shop need?

It depends on the size, ceiling height, layout and how even you want the sound. Several smaller speakers usually give better coverage than one or two large ones. Your contractor can suggest a layout after seeing the space.

Can I use my phone and a Bluetooth speaker?

It can work for a very small space, but it is less reliable and gives uneven coverage. Check the terms of any music app for business use, and consider a permanent system if music is a regular part of your shop.

Do I need separate volume for each area?

Not always. A single open room can work with one zone. Separate controls help when areas differ, such as seating, fitting rooms and staff areas. More zones mean more equipment and wiring, so decide what you really need.

Who is responsible for music licensing?

The shop operator is usually responsible for checking that music played in the premises is properly permitted. Ask your music provider what applies to public playback, and keep records. This is general guidance, not legal advice.
