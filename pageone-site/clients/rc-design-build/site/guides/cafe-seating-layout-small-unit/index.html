Cafe Seating Layout for a Small Unit

Plan cafe seating from the flow outward: where customers queue, order, collect and sit, and where staff carry food and clear plates. Fix the counter and service route first, then fit a mix of seat types around clear walkways. Mark the plan in tape and act out a busy hour before any joinery is ordered.

Where should I start when planning cafe seating?

Start with the route a customer and a member of staff each take through the cafe, not with the number of chairs. A seating plan works when people can enter, queue, order, collect, sit, leave and clear a table without crossing each other.

  1. Mark the door, the counter and the pickup point on a scaled sketch of the unit.
  2. Draw the customer route from door to order to pickup to seat.
  3. Draw the staff route from kitchen or bar to tables and to the dish return.
  4. Only then place tables and seats in the space that is left.

If the two routes cross at a busy point, move the counter, the pickup point or a table before anything is built.

How many seats can a small cafe fit?

There is no single number, because it depends on the unit, the door position, the counter size and how much walkway you need. Treat seat counts from other cafes as a rough guide and confirm them against your own floor plan. Fewer comfortable seats usually serve a small cafe better than a crowded room where people knock chairs and trays.

Tape the table and chair positions on the floor, then ask a few people to sit, stand up and walk through with a tray. Note every spot where someone has to turn sideways or wait, and remove a seat there.

Seating limits, exits and fire safety depend on your building and use. Confirm them with your landlord, building management or the relevant authority before the plan is final.

Which mix of seat types works best?

A mix of seat types lets a small cafe serve solo customers, pairs and small groups without leaving odd gaps. Choose the mix from who you expect to serve, then check that each type has room to be used comfortably.

  • Bar or window-counter seats suit solo customers and use a narrow strip of floor.
  • Two-seat tables suit pairs and can be pushed together for a group.
  • Banquette or bench seating along a wall fits more people into the same length but is harder to change later.
  • A few movable chairs keep the plan flexible when the cafe is busy or quiet.

Think about bags, prams and coats as well as people. A small hook or shelf at each seat keeps them off the floor and out of the walkway.

How wide should the walkways be?

Walkways should let one person pass another who is sitting down, and let staff carry a tray without touching a chair back. The exact width depends on your unit, so confirm it on site with a taped mock-up instead of relying on a figure from a different layout.

  • Keep the route from the door to the counter clear of tables and chairs.
  • Leave room for a chair to be pulled out without blocking the walkway.
  • Give the dish return and the bin area their own access so they do not sit in the main aisle.
  • Keep any exit route clear at all times.

Where should the order counter and pickup point go?

Put the order counter where customers can see it from the door, and put the pickup point to one side so people collecting drinks do not block people ordering. Queue space should be inside the cafe, not across the seating.

The counter itself is a joinery job that sets the whole plan, so decide its length, height and the position of the till, display and handover before you place tables. Our guide to planning a custom shop counter covers what the counter needs to hold, and the cafe and service counter fit-out page shows how we approach this kind of space.

How do I plan storage and clearing?

Plan where used cups, plates and trays go as carefully as where food comes out. A clear dish-return point keeps tables free and the walkways open.

  • Place the dish return close to the kitchen or wash area, away from the entrance.
  • Give staff a shelf or trolley position near the seating for trays and cleaning items.
  • Keep back-of-house storage for stock out of the customer view.
  • Leave a little spare room, because stock and equipment tend to grow.

Built-in storage is carpentry work; see commercial carpentry for what it normally covers.

Which surfaces and lighting suit a busy cafe?

Choose floors, table tops and wall areas that wipe clean, resist spills and cope with chairs moving all day. Smooth, sealed surfaces are easier to look after than porous ones, and tight joints keep crumbs and liquid out of gaps.

Light the seating warmly enough to feel relaxed, and make sure the counter and pickup point are brighter so staff and customers can see what they are doing. Check samples under the lights you plan to use. The retail display lighting guide explains layers, glare and colour rendering, and the same ideas apply to a cafe. For floors, see commercial tiling and flooring.

What should I prepare before a site assessment?

Bring a floor plan or sketch, photos of the unit, a list of what you will serve and the seat mix you have in mind. Kitchen, water, drainage and ventilation positions are specialist matters, so raise them at the site assessment and confirm what the building allows.

Walk your taped mock-up with your team first and note where it felt tight. Then send us your floor plan so we can discuss your scope.

Frequently asked questions

How do I plan a cafe layout in a small space?

Start with the customer and staff routes, fix the counter and pickup point, then fit seating around clear walkways. Mark everything in tape on the floor and act out a busy hour before ordering any joinery.

Should I choose banquette seating or loose chairs?

Banquettes fit more people along a wall and look tidy, but they are fixed. Loose chairs cost less to change and move around. Many small cafes use a banquette on the longest wall and loose chairs elsewhere.

Where should the pickup point go in a cafe?

To one side of the order counter, so people collecting drinks do not block people ordering. Keep it visible from the queue and out of the main walkway.

Do I need a drawing before talking to a contractor?

A rough sketch, photos of the unit and a list of what you will serve are enough to start. A contractor can then check dimensions on site, discuss the layout and flag anything that needs landlord or building approval.
