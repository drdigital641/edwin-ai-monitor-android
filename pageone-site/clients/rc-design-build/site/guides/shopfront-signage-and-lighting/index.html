Shopfront Signage and Lighting

Plan shopfront signage from the street inward: where passers-by first see your shop, how far away they read it, and what lights it after dark. Choose the sign type, then plan power, wiring and lighting together, and confirm size and position with your landlord before anything is made.

What should a shopfront sign do?

A shopfront sign should tell passers-by what the shop is and make them want to look inside, at the distance and angle they actually see it from. The name matters, but so does a simple cue about what you sell.

  • Stand across the walkway or corridor and note where people first look up.
  • Check what blocks the view: pillars, trees, covered walkways or the neighbour’s sign.
  • Keep the message short enough to read in a glance.

Decide the position from these sightlines, not from where the wall happens to have space.

Which types of shopfront sign are there?

Most shopfront signs are one of a few types. Each has a different look, a different way of being lit and a different amount of joinery, wiring and fixing behind it.

  • A fascia sign sits flat above the door or window and is the most common choice.
  • A projecting or hanging sign sticks out from the wall so people walking along the frontage can see it from the side.
  • Individual letters fixed to the wall give a clean, modern look and can be lit from behind or from the front.
  • A lightbox or panel gives even light across the whole sign face.
  • A window graphic uses the glass itself and suits a short name, hours or a simple message.

Pick the type that suits the frontage, then plan how it is fixed and powered.

How should storefront lighting be planned?

Plan storefront lighting in two jobs: light that shows the sign, and light that shows the display behind the glass. They should support each other rather than compete, so a bright sign does not wash out the window and a bright window does not make the sign look dull.

  • Light the sign evenly, with no hot spots or dark patches.
  • Put warmer, softer light on the window display so products look inviting.
  • Check the shopfront in daylight, at dusk and at night, because it changes through the day.

The retail display lighting guide explains layers, glare and colour rendering inside the shop, and the same ideas apply at the window. Our feature lighting page covers how lighting is planned across a space.

How do I avoid glare and reflections on the glass?

Glass reflects whatever is brightest in front of it, so a poorly placed light or a sunny wall opposite can hide your display. Test the view from the places customers will stand.

  • Angle display lights so their reflection does not land in the viewer’s eyes.
  • Keep interior light on the display brighter than the reflected scene, or the glass turns into a mirror.
  • Avoid glossy finishes right beside the glass that bounce light across it.
  • Look at the shopfront at different times of day before fixing positions.

What power and wiring does a sign need?

An illuminated sign needs a power supply, a switch or timer and a neat cable route to the sign. Planning this early avoids cables on show and cuts into finished walls or ceilings later.

  • Decide where the sign will be, then where the power point and switch or timer go.
  • Plan a concealed cable route and a safe, accessible point to service the sign.
  • Leave a little spare capacity in case you add a second sign or a display light later.

Electrical work should be done by a qualified person. See commercial electrical works for what it normally covers.

Which materials and finishes last on a shopfront?

A shopfront sign faces dust, rain-splashed air, heat and daily cleaning, so choose materials that hold their finish. Smooth, sealed surfaces are easier to wipe clean, and tight joints keep dirt and water out of gaps.

  • Choose finishes that will not fade or peel quickly under bright light.
  • Check that fixings are suitable for the wall behind and can carry the weight of the sign.
  • Ask how the sign can be opened or removed for repair without damaging the wall.

A timber or board-backed sign panel is carpentry work; see commercial carpentry.

What should I confirm with my landlord first?

Confirm the size, position, lighting and fixing method of your sign with your landlord or building management before anything is made. Rules for shopfronts and signs differ between buildings and landlords, and a sign that is made first and rejected later is expensive to redo.

  • Ask what size and position are allowed, and whether lit signs are permitted.
  • Ask what you must submit, such as a drawing or sample, and how long approval takes.
  • Keep the written approval with your quotation.

Our small shop renovation checklist lists the other approvals to ask about, and it is worth reading before you commit to a design.

What should I prepare before a site assessment?

Bring photos of the shopfront from across the walkway, a rough sketch with the sign position you have in mind, your logo and any landlord guidance you have. Ask the contractor to check the wall, the power position and the view on site.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most common type of shopfront sign?

A fascia sign, which sits flat above the door or window. Projecting signs and individual wall letters are common alternatives, and the right choice depends on the frontage and how people approach the shop.

Should a shopfront sign be lit?

Lit signs are easier to read after dark, but not every landlord or building allows every type of lighting. Check first, then plan the power and switch or timer along with the sign.

Why can I not see my window display during the day?

Glare and reflections from the brighter scene outside can hide it. Brighter, well-angled light on the display and a check of the view from where customers stand usually helps.

Do I need landlord approval for a shopfront sign?

Often yes, but rules differ between buildings and landlords. Ask for the allowed size, position and lighting, and what to submit, before you order a sign.
