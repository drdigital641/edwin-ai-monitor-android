Lighting a Retail Display

Light a retail display in layers: ambient light for general visibility, accent light to draw the eye, and display light inside or on fixtures. Then choose colour temperature and colour rendering to suit your products, control glare through aiming, and add dimming so the mood can change through the day.

Planning guide · 6 min read · Published 24 October 2026

What are the layers of retail lighting?

Most shops use three layers: ambient light that fills the space, accent light that draws attention to chosen items or surfaces, and display light built into shelves or cabinets. Combining them gives depth and guides customers without relying on one bright, flat source.

  • Ambient: general light so people can walk, read signs and see the whole space comfortably.
  • Accent: focused light on feature products, walls, signage or a focal point.
  • Display: light within or on shelving, showcases and counters, so products are lit at close range.

Plan the layers together. If the ambient layer is too bright, accent lighting loses its effect; if it is too dim, the shop feels closed and products are hard to read.

Which colour temperature should I choose?

Choose a colour temperature that suits your products and brand, then keep it consistent across the shop. Warmer light feels cosy and flatters timber, leather and skin tones, while cooler light feels crisp and clean. Mixing different temperatures randomly makes a space look uneven.

LookCommon starting point
Warm, invitingAround 2,700-3,000 K
Neutral, balancedAround 3,500-4,000 K
Cool, crispAround 4,000 K and above

These are common starting points, not rules. Test samples against your actual products and finishes before ordering.

Why does colour rendering matter?

Colour rendering describes how faithfully a light source shows the colours of objects. For products where colour matters, such as clothing, cosmetics, food or jewellery, better colour rendering helps items look as customers expect and can reduce returns caused by colour surprises.

  • Check the colour rendering information given for each lamp or fitting.
  • Compare samples of your key products under the proposed light, not only a catalogue.
  • Keep the same type of light across neighbouring displays, so colours look consistent.
  • Check how fabrics and skin tones look, not just packaging.

Ask suppliers to explain what the specification means in practice, and view a sample in the shop or a similar setting if possible.

How do I avoid glare?

Avoid glare by hiding the light source from normal viewing positions, using shielded or recessed fittings, and aiming light away from eye level and glossy surfaces. Glare makes a shop uncomfortable and hides the products you want customers to see.

  • Check sight lines from the entrance, the counter and common standing points.
  • Be careful with glass, mirrors, polished stone and glossy packaging.
  • Use shields, snoots or deeper recessed fittings where needed.
  • Keep bright accent sources from shining into customers' eyes when they look at a feature.

Look at the display from a customer's height and from several angles. A layout that looks good in a drawing can still glare in person.

How should accent lights be aimed?

Aim accent lights so the beam lands on the product or surface and not on the floor or the customer. Adjustable track or spot fittings let you refine aiming after products are in place, which is the practical way to reach a good result.

  1. Install and place your products or merchandise first.
  2. Switch on accent lights and view from the customer's position.
  3. Adjust angle and beam width so light falls on the item, not behind it.
  4. Check for shadows cast by shelves, signs or people standing nearby.
  5. Re-aim whenever a display changes.

Beam width matters as much as direction: narrow beams create drama on a single item, while wider beams suit a group of products or a wall. Mounting positions should be confirmed on site, since ceiling height, beams and ducts can limit where fittings can go.

What are common lighting mistakes in small shops?

The common mistakes are lighting only the ceiling, mixing unrelated colour temperatures, leaving shelves in shadow, pointing lights at glass and forgetting to plan for changes. Most of these can be avoided by deciding what you want to highlight, and how customers will see it, before you choose any fittings.

  • Relying on one general light and expecting products to stand out.
  • Lighting the middle of the shop but not the walls, where many products sit.
  • Placing lights directly above shelves, so the lowest products fall in shadow.
  • Choosing fittings by appearance and ignoring beam angle and heat.
  • Leaving no spare power points or circuits for seasonal displays.

Walk the space in daylight and at night, if possible, because shopfront glass and neighbouring units change what the lighting looks like. Daylight at the entrance may mean the first display needs more accent light than you expect.

Also consider maintenance. Ask how lamps or modules are replaced, how high the fittings are and whether staff can reach them safely. A neat design that is difficult to maintain tends to be neglected.

Why add dimming and controls?

Dimming and scene controls let you adjust brightness for opening hours, daylight, promotions and cleaning, and can make the shop feel different in the morning and the evening. They also let you fine-tune levels after installation, when you can see the real effect.

  • Group lights by layer, so ambient, accent and display can be changed separately.
  • Choose fittings and drivers that are compatible with the dimming method.
  • Keep controls simple enough for staff to use without help.
  • Plan wall controls or an app only where they will actually be used.

Compatibility between lights and controls is a common source of problems, so confirm it before ordering.

What should I prepare before planning lighting?

Bring a floor plan, ceiling photos, a list of products to highlight, the colours and materials in the shop and your target opening date. General lighting installation and power points are usually part of electrical works, while feature and display lighting with controls sits in the lighting scope.

Frequently asked questions

Is brighter lighting better for a shop?

Not necessarily. Too much even brightness flattens a display and wastes the effect of accent lighting. Aim for enough ambient light for comfort, then use contrast to highlight what matters. Test the result in the shop with real products.

Can I use the same lights for ambient and display?

Sometimes, in a very small shop, but separate layers usually give better results. Ambient fittings are chosen for spread and comfort, while accent and display lights are chosen for beam, colour rendering and aiming flexibility.

Should every light be dimmable?

Not always. Dimming is most useful for accent and mood lighting, where levels change through the day. Check that lights and controls are compatible, and keep the control system simple enough for your staff to use.

Do I need to change lights when I change my displays?

Often only the aiming, not the fittings. Adjustable accent lights let you re-aim when products move. Planning flexibility at the start, with track or movable fittings, usually makes later changes easier.
