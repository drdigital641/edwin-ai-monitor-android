Rim, brake caliper and engine bay painting: what to know first

Rims, calipers and the engine bay are small areas that change how the whole car looks. Here is what each job involves, what makes it last, and how to choose colours that suit the car.

By MCS Auto Spray Painting · Updated 22 November 2026 · 5-minute read

WhatsApp us a photoCall

Why people paint the details

There are usually two reasons. The first is repair: rims scraped on kerbs, rim paint that has faded or started to peel, calipers stained brown by years of brake dust, or an engine bay that looks tired every time the bonnet opens. The second is style: a contrast colour on the calipers that shows through the spokes, darker rims for a sportier look, or an engine bay painted to match a new body colour. Both are worth doing well, because these are the parts people look at closely.

Rims: repair first, then paint

Kerb rash, the scrapes along the outer edge of a rim, is the most common rim problem in town driving and tight parking. Light kerb rash can usually be sanded smooth, filled where needed and repainted so the scrape disappears. The rim is cleaned and degreased, because brake dust and road grime stop paint from sticking, then prepped, painted and finished with clear coat for gloss and protection.

Rim colours range from factory silver, gunmetal and black to bright contrast colours. Two-tone finishes, for example a dark face with a polished or contrast lip, take more masking and time. If you want to keep the original look, tell the painter so the colour and gloss can be matched to the other wheels.

Painting is not a fix for structural damage. A rim that is bent, cracked or wobbles at speed is a safety issue and should be checked by a wheel and tyre specialist before any cosmetic work is done.

Brake calipers: heat, dust and masking

Brakes get very hot, so calipers need paint that can handle heat; ordinary paint can discolour or peel. Before painting, the caliper has to be cleaned and degreased thoroughly, because brake dust is fine and greasy. The parts that must never get paint on them, the brake disc, the pads, rubber seals, sliders and bleed nipples, are masked off carefully.

Calipers can be painted on the car with the wheels removed, or taken off for a full refinish. If a caliper is removed, the brakes have to be refitted correctly and bled afterwards, which is safety work and should never be rushed. Ask the workshop which method they use for your car and why.

Popular caliper colours are red, yellow, blue and silver, but the best choice depends on the body and rim colours. A bright caliper behind dark rims stands out; the same colour behind silver rims is more subtle.

Engine bays: lots of masking

Engine-bay painting takes more preparation than any other small job, because the bay is full of hoses, wiring, sensors, labels and rubber seals that must be protected or removed. It is most often done as part of a full colour change, so the bay matches the new body colour when the bonnet is open, along with the door jambs and boot opening. It is also done on show cars and restorations where a clean, painted bay is part of the look.

If you are changing colour, decide early whether the engine bay, door jambs and boot opening will be painted too. Leaving them in the old colour is cheaper, but the old colour shows every time a door or the bonnet opens. Remember that a full colour change must be updated with JPJ; our respray guide explains the steps.

Choosing colours that work together

Look at the car as a whole: body colour, rim colour, caliper colour and any trim or body-kit parts. Two or three colours that work together look deliberate; too many look random. It helps to see real painted samples next to each other rather than images on a screen. Our colour lounge at the workshop has walls of spray-out samples, including solids, metallics, candy, pearl and colour-shift finishes, so you can hold a sample next to the car.

If you are also adding a body kit or carbon-fibre parts, plan the colours together so the rims, calipers and kit match. See our carbon-fibre and body kit page for that side of the job.

Looking after painted rims and calipers

  • Rinse brake dust off regularly with a gentle wheel shampoo
  • Avoid strong acid wheel cleaners, which can dull the finish
  • Use a separate soft brush for wheels so grit does not reach the body paint
  • Touch in chips early, before moisture gets under the paint
  • Take care with kerbs when parking, especially with a fresh finish

What to ask before you book

  • Will the rims be repaired and prepped before painting, or just painted over?
  • Is the caliper paint suitable for heat?
  • Which parts of the brakes will be masked, and will the calipers be removed?
  • For an engine bay, what will be removed or masked?
  • Will the finish have clear coat?

Questions about this topic

Can kerb-scuffed rims be repaired?

Light kerb rash can usually be sanded, filled and repainted. A rim that is bent or cracked is a safety issue and should be checked by a wheel specialist first.

What paint is used on brake calipers?

Calipers need paint that can handle brake heat, applied after thorough cleaning and degreasing, with the disc, pads, seals and bleed nipples masked off.

Do I need to tell JPJ if I paint my rims or calipers?

Painting rims or calipers does not change the body colour. A full body colour change does need to be updated with JPJ.
