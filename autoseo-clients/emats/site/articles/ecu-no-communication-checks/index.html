ECU no communication with the scan tool? What to check before you blame the module

11 Nov 2026 · 3 min read · EMATS, Johor Bahru

WhatsApp us Call +60 10-820 2063

When a car shows ECU no communication on the scan tool, the module itself is only one of the possible causes. Power and earth to the ECU, a blown fuse or relay, the diagnostic socket and the CAN bus wiring all give the same symptom. Ruling those out first saves your customer a module that didn't need repairing, and tells you when it's time to send the ECU in.

> Short answer

No communication with the ECU is often a supply or network fault, not a dead module. Check battery voltage, the ECU fuses and main relay, power and earth at the ECU connector, the diagnostic socket and the CAN bus. If those are all good and the other modules talk but the ECU still doesn't, the ECU is worth checking on the bench.

01

First, does anything on the car talk?

Start by asking whether the problem is the ECU alone or the whole network. That one answer decides where you look next:

No module answers at all
Look at the diagnostic socket, its power and earth, and the scan tool and lead before the ECU.
Other modules answer, the ECU doesn't
Look at the ECU's own fuses, main relay, power, earth and its CAN connection.
The ECU answers sometimes
Look for a loose connector, a poor earth or corrosion, and note whether heat or vibration changes it.
The ECU answers but the car won't start
A different fault: read the codes and check the sensor, injector and ignition circuits it reports.

02

The checks to do before you remove the ECU

These are standard checks, and most of them only need a multimeter and the wiring diagram for the car:

  1. 01Battery voltage with the ignition on, and again while cranking: a low supply can stop the ECU from waking up
  2. 02Every fuse and the main relay that feed the ECU, tested under load rather than by eye
  3. 03Power and earth at the ECU connector itself, with the connector plugged in where you can back-probe it
  4. 04The diagnostic socket: on an OBD-II socket, pin 16 should have battery voltage, and pins 4 and 5 should be good earths
  5. 05The CAN bus: with the battery disconnected, a healthy high-speed CAN bus usually reads about 60 ohms between pins 6 and 14 at the socket
  6. 06The ECU connector and pins for water, green corrosion, pushed-back pins or signs of a previous repair

03

Signs the ECU itself is the fault

When the supply, earth and network checks all pass, these are the signs that point back to the module. They match the reasons workshops usually send an ECU to our bench:

  • Good power and earth at the connector, but the ECU still doesn't answer
  • The other modules on the same bus answer normally
  • Water damage, corrosion or burnt parts visible on the board or in the case
  • Cut-outs or lost communication that follow the module, not the wiring
  • Faults that keep returning after the wiring has been checked and repaired

Questions workshops ask

Can a flat or weak battery cause ECU no communication?

Yes. If the supply drops too low, the ECU may not wake up or may drop off the network while cranking. Check battery voltage with the ignition on and while cranking before anything else.

What should a CAN bus read between pins 6 and 14?

On most cars with high-speed CAN, about 60 ohms with the battery disconnected, because two 120 ohm resistors sit at the ends of the bus. A reading far from that points to a wiring or termination problem rather than one module.

Do you need the whole car to check the ECU?

No. Your workshop removes the ECU and sends or drops it off. We check it on the bench and tell you the fault and the cost before any repair.
